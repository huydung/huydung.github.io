# Statistical Tests

Statistical tests provide formal procedures for hypothesis testing, estimating effect sizes, and quantifying confidence in findings. From simple comparisons between groups (t-tests, ANOVA) to complex structural relationships (regression, mediation, moderation), statistical tests translate research questions into testable propositions and reveal whether observed patterns are likely due to real effects or chance variation.

## T-Test (2 Groups)

Compares means on a continuous variable between two independent groups, testing whether the difference is significantly different from zero. The t-statistic reflects the ratio of group difference to within-group variability. In an organizational study, a t-test might compare employee engagement scores between teams with high versus low psychological safety, determining whether engagement differs significantly across safety levels.

## ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)

Tests whether means differ significantly across three or more groups by comparing between-group variance to within-group variance. ANOVA is appropriate when you have one continuous outcome and one categorical predictor with more than two levels. An organization might use ANOVA to compare outcomes across three departments or four team types, testing whether structural position affects performance or engagement.

## One-Way ANOVA

The simplest ANOVA variant: one continuous outcome, one categorical predictor (factor) with multiple levels, all observations independent. The F-statistic tests whether group means differ. In an organizational study, one-way ANOVA might compare employee engagement across three psychological safety levels (low, moderate, high), answering whether safety-level group membership predicts engagement.

## Two-Way ANOVA

Examines effects of two categorical predictors (factors) on a continuous outcome, testing main effects and interactions. Two-way ANOVA reveals not only whether each factor affects the outcome, but whether the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. In organizational research, a two-way ANOVA might examine how team size (small/large) and manager experience (novice/experienced) interact to predict team cohesion—revealing, for example, that manager experience matters more in larger teams.

## N-Way ANOVA

Extends the two-way framework to three or more categorical predictors, examining their main effects and all possible interactions. N-way ANOVA is powerful but complex; interpretation becomes challenging with many factors and interactions. In comprehensive organizational studies, N-way ANOVA might examine how cultural background, team size, and management style jointly influence team outcomes.

## F-Statistic

The test statistic in ANOVA, calculated as the ratio of between-group mean square to within-group mean square. Larger F-values indicate greater between-group differences relative to within-group variation, suggesting that the categorical predictor meaningfully explains variation in the outcome. In an organizational study, a large F-statistic indicates that team type or department significantly explains performance variation.

## Post-Hoc Tests

After a significant ANOVA, post-hoc tests (e.g., Tukey, Scheffe, Bonferroni) conduct pairwise comparisons between groups while controlling for Type I error inflation from multiple comparisons. Post-hoc tests answer: Which specific groups differ from which? In a one-way ANOVA comparing three teams' performance outcomes, a significant overall F might be followed by Tukey tests revealing that the high-performing team differs significantly from both low-performing teams, but they don't differ from each other.

## Pearson Correlation

Measures the strength and direction of linear association between two continuous variables, ranging from -1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive), with 0 indicating no linear relationship. Pearson r assumes continuous variables and linear relationships. In organizational research, Pearson correlations might quantify relationships—organizational culture correlating with employee engagement (r ≈ 0.41), and engagement correlating with performance (r ≈ 0.54), establishing foundational evidence for mediation models.

## Spearman Correlation

A non-parametric alternative to Pearson that ranks data and computes correlation on ranks, useful when linearity assumptions are violated or variables are ordinal. Spearman rho ranges from -1 to +1 like Pearson r but doesn't assume normality or linearity. In organizational studies where some variables (e.g., team size, tenure) are ordinal or non-normally distributed, Spearman correlations provide robust estimates of association.

## Chi-Square Test

Tests association between two categorical variables by comparing observed cell frequencies to expected frequencies under independence. Chi-square is appropriate when both variables are categorical (nominal or ordinal). In organizational research, chi-square might examine whether psychological safety category (low/medium/high) is associated with team satisfaction category (dissatisfied/neutral/satisfied), revealing whether safety and satisfaction co-occur.

## Simple Regression

Predicts a continuous outcome from a single continuous predictor using the linear equation Y = a + bX + e. The regression coefficient b quantifies the change in Y per unit increase in X. In organizational research, simple regression might predict employee performance from organizational engagement alone, asking: "For every one-unit increase in engagement, how much does performance increase?"

## Multiple Regression

Extends simple regression to multiple predictors (Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + ... + e), allowing control for confounders and examination of unique effects. Each coefficient represents the unique effect of that predictor, holding others constant. Organizational studies often employ multiple regression to predict performance from psychological safety while controlling for covariates (e.g., tenure, role, experience), isolating the unique safety effect.

## Confidence Intervals and Standard Error

A confidence interval (e.g., 95% CI) is a range around a point estimate (mean, correlation, coefficient) that likely contains the true population parameter. Standard error quantifies the variability of a statistic across repeated samples; wider standard errors indicate less precision. In an analysis, the relationship coefficient (β=0.537) would be accompanied by a 95% confidence interval (e.g., [0.421, 0.653]), indicating the range of plausible population values.

## P-Value

The probability of observing a test statistic as extreme as or more extreme than the one obtained, given that the null hypothesis is true. A p-value < 0.05 conventionally signals statistical significance, rejecting H₀. However, p-values are often misinterpreted; a significant p-value indicates that the observed effect is unlikely under the null, but does not measure effect size or practical importance. In analysis, a relationship with p < 0.001 provides strong evidence against the null of no relationship.

## Mediation Analysis

Tests whether a predictor's effect on an outcome is transmitted through an intermediate variable (mediator). Mediation analysis partitions the total effect into direct effect (X → Y) and indirect effect (X → M → Y). The VAF (Variance Accounted For) by the indirect effect quantifies mediation strength. In organizational research, mediation analysis might test: Does psychological safety mediate culture's effect on performance? A significant indirect pathway and substantial VAF would confirm that safety partially explains the culture-performance link.

## Moderation Analysis

Tests whether the relationship between a predictor and outcome differs across levels of a moderator variable. Moderation examines interaction effects: "Does the effect of X on Y depend on M?" Moderation analysis involves testing X × M as a predictor of Y; significant interaction coefficients indicate moderation. In organizational studies, moderation analysis might ask: Does team experience moderate the relationship between psychological safety and performance? (e.g., Does safety matter more for new teams than experienced ones?)

## Tools: R Commander, SPSS, SmartPLS

**R Commander**: A point-and-click graphical interface to the R statistical environment, offering user-friendly access to hundreds of statistical tests, visualizations, and modeling capabilities. R Commander reduces programming barriers while leveraging R's power and flexibility. Researchers comfortable with R but wanting faster interaction often use R Commander.

**SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences)**: Industry-standard proprietary software with extensive menus for descriptive statistics, inferential testing, regression, and factor analysis. SPSS is widely taught in academic programs and used in organizational research; its accessibility to non-programmers makes it popular for survey-based studies.

**SmartPLS**: Specialized software for Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), designed for complex mediation, moderation, and latent variable models. SmartPLS offers intuitive graphical interface for path modeling, bootstrapping for confidence intervals, and built-in mediation analysis features for testing sophisticated theoretical models.
