# Qualitative Iterative Process

Qualitative analysis unfolds iteratively and organically, with coding, memoing, and pattern identification happening simultaneously with data collection. Unlike the linear quantitative pipeline, qualitative work is recursive: insights from early interviews shape later sampling, early codes spawn new questions, and emerging themes prompt additional data gathering. This responsiveness allows researchers to pursue unanticipated dimensions and deepen understanding as patterns surface.

## Simultaneous Collection and Analysis

Begin coding and memoing while data collection is underway, rather than waiting until all interviews or observations are complete. Early analysis shapes sampling direction, interview probes, and research focus. In a qualitative study of team dynamics, identifying early codes around "voice" and "risk" in initial interviews might prompt deeper probing in subsequent interviews about moments when team members felt safe or unsafe speaking up.

## Coding–Memoing–Pattern Cycles

Move fluidly among three activities: assigning codes to data segments, writing analytical memos to capture thoughts and connections, and identifying larger patterns and relationships. Each cycle refines the previous, building analytical depth. An iterative approach might start with 300+ initial codes assigned exploratorily to interview segments, progress to memoing on key themes, then consolidate into 50 focused categories and finally 7-8 integrated themes that explain the phenomenon holistically.

## Five Analytic Strategies (Patton)

### Inductive Analysis
Derive themes and categories directly from the data without imposing prior frameworks, allowing patterns to emerge naturally. In qualitative interviews about team dynamics, letting codes emerge from what respondents actually say—rather than forcing responses into predetermined categories—respects the complexity and authenticity of their experience.

### Creative Synthesis
Make novel connections across disparate data segments, drawing on metaphor, analogy, and imaginative interpretation to generate new insights. When coding interview data on organizational culture, a researcher might notice parallels between "iterative team problem-solving" and "iterative personal development," synthesizing these into a novel framework for understanding team evolution.

### Cause-and-Effect Analysis
Identify temporal and logical sequences showing how conditions, actions, and outcomes relate. Interview narratives often reveal causal chains: "X happened, then Y responded, which led to Z outcome." In studies of organizational change, analyzing how events sequence reveals turning points when team dynamics shift.

### Context Sensitivity
Situate findings within the organizational, cultural, and temporal context in which they occur, recognizing that meaning is context-dependent. A statement about "risk-taking" means something different in a newly formed startup versus a decade-old established firm; coding must account for context. Understanding team safety requires attention to organizational culture and norms specific to that setting.

### Unique Case Orientation
Preserve attention to what makes each case distinct while also drawing cross-case patterns. Rather than dissolving individual narratives into abstract categories, honor particularity. If one team member's journey to voicing concerns followed a unique trajectory, that particularity enriches the overall thematic picture and might highlight that organizational safety is built through diverse pathways.

## Integration into Coherent Themes

Through successive coding cycles and analytic memoing, disparate codes and patterns converge into a smaller set of integrated themes that explain the phenomenon holistically. Consolidating from hundreds of initial codes to dozens of focused codes to 7-8 final themes—each articulating a core aspect of the phenomenon—makes findings actionable and memorable for stakeholders.
