## Types of Literature Reviews

Literature reviews vary in scope, methodology, and purpose. Different types suit different research questions and contexts. A research program might employ elements of several types: narrative review to establish the conceptual landscape, systematic review to rigorously evaluate evidence on specific relationships, and argumentative review to build the case for an intervention study. Understanding these types helps you select the most appropriate approach for your goals.

## Narrative (Traditional) Review
A narrative review synthesizes existing literature to describe the state of knowledge on a topic, identifying themes, gaps, and contradictions without following a rigid systematic protocol. It is flexible, exploratory, and well-suited to broad questions or emerging fields. Narrative reviews often have subjective elements—the author's judgment about which sources are important shapes the synthesis. Strengths: comprehensive, flexible, good for identifying big-picture patterns. Weaknesses: less transparent in source selection, potential for bias, difficult to replicate.

## Systematic Review
A systematic review follows a pre-specified, rigorous protocol to identify, evaluate, and synthesize all available evidence on a focused question. It begins with a clear RQ, defines inclusion/exclusion criteria, searches multiple databases exhaustively, evaluates study quality using standardized tools, and synthesizes findings using explicit methods. Systematic reviews minimize bias and provide high-quality evidence summaries. Strengths: transparent, comprehensive, high rigor. Weaknesses: labor-intensive, requires precise RQ, works best for narrow questions.

## Meta-Analysis
Meta-analysis is a statistical synthesis of multiple quantitative studies, combining effect sizes to estimate the overall strength of a relationship. It requires studies with comparable measures and designs. Meta-analysis enables evidence-based conclusions about which relationships are robust versus context-dependent. Strengths: quantifies overall effect, identifies moderators, high statistical power. Weaknesses: requires comparable studies, sensitive to publication bias, assumes effect sizes are meaningfully comparable.

## Scoping Review
A scoping review maps the landscape of literature on a broad topic, identifying types of research, conceptual boundaries, and gaps—without assessing study quality in detail. It answers: "What research exists on topic X? What are the key themes? What are the gaps?" Scoping reviews are exploratory and suitable for emerging fields. Strengths: comprehensive overview, identifies research gaps. Weaknesses: less rigorous than systematic review, descriptive rather than evaluative.

## Argumentative Review
An argumentative review critically evaluates sources to build a persuasive case for a particular position or conclusion. It selects sources strategically to support a thesis and may directly critique sources that contradict the argument. Argumentative reviews can be powerful but risk confirmation bias if sources are selected to support a predetermined conclusion. Strengths: persuasive, positions research clearly. Weaknesses: potential for bias, less transparent in source selection.

## Theoretical Review
A theoretical review synthesizes literature on a particular theory or conceptual framework, tracing its evolution, critiquing its assumptions, and proposing refinements. It examines foundational work, contemporary developments, and critiques, positioning new research as extending theory. Theoretical reviews deepen understanding of conceptual frameworks and often reveal hidden assumptions. Strengths: rigorous conceptual analysis, reveals assumptions. Weaknesses: may be less empirically grounded, requires strong theoretical expertise.

## Mixed-Type Approach
Most research projects employ elements of multiple review types. A comprehensive literature review might be primarily narrative in structure (mapping the field, building the case), include systematic elements (searching databases, applying quality criteria), and incorporate theoretical review (critiquing assumptions). This mixed approach provides both breadth (understanding the landscape) and depth (rigorously evaluating key relationships).
