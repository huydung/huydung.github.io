# Sampling Techniques

Sampling techniques fall into two broad families: probability sampling (random, systematic, stratified, cluster) for quantitative research aiming at statistical generalization, and non-probability sampling (convenience, purposive, snowball, quota) for targeted, often qualitative research emphasizing information-richness over representativeness. Probability methods assign each population member a known, non-zero chance of selection, enabling unbiased estimation and generalization; non-probability methods rely on judgment or convenience, introducing potential bias but offering practical flexibility. Most organizational research blends both: a probability base (e.g., stratified random sampling of studios across regions) combined with non-probability refinement (e.g., purposefully oversampling junior developers to ensure their voices are heard).

Strategic combination of sampling techniques leverages strengths of both. Quantitative research often uses stratified probability sampling to ensure representation across organizational levels, departments, and geographies. Qualitative research uses purposive non-probability sampling—deliberately recruiting diverse participants—to maximize depth and insight. The choice of technique shapes what claims can be made: probability sampling supports claims about population prevalence and generalization; purposive sampling supports claims about mechanisms and lived experiences.
