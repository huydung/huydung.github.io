# Sampling Errors & Bias

Sampling introduces unavoidable error and potential bias. Sampling error is the natural statistical variation between a sample statistic and the true population parameter—if you repeatedly sampled from a population, each sample would yield slightly different means or effect sizes. Sampling error decreases with larger samples and is quantifiable (captured in confidence intervals and standard errors). Bias, by contrast, is systematic error from faulty design or execution—if your sample is systematically different from the population in ways related to your outcomes, conclusions will be systematically wrong. Types of sampling bias include selection error (who participates), nonresponse error (who declines), sample frame error (omissions from the population list), and specification error (measuring the wrong variable). Understanding and mitigating these errors is central to credible research.

Effective sampling acknowledges both sources of error. Sampling error is unavoidable but manageable through adequate sample size and statistical inference. Bias, however, undermines validity and must be actively mitigated through design choices like randomization, diverse recruitment methods, non-response follow-up, and sensitivity checks. Awareness of these error types and deliberate mitigation strengthen research credibility.

## Population-Specific Error & Selection Error

Population-specific error arises when you measure the wrong population or define your population too broadly or narrowly. Example: If you aim to understand psychological safety across all tech workers but sample only from large established companies, findings may not generalize to indie studios, outsourcing firms, or startup contexts—different organizational cultures, compensation levels, and stability affect safety differently.

Selection error occurs when your recruitment process systematically includes or excludes certain types of people. Example: Recruiting participants by email during work hours may skew toward office-based employees and exclude remote workers; recruiting through "employee ambassadors" may skew toward well-liked, socially integrated employees and exclude isolated or introverted workers. Selection error biases results if excluded groups differ on the outcome (e.g., if isolated employees feel less safe, excluding them inflates safety scores).

## Nonresponse Error

Nonresponse error arises when invited participants decline to participate. If non-respondents differ from respondents on key dimensions, results are biased. Example: If employees who had recent negative leadership experiences decline to complete surveys (due to fear or distrust), the sample over-represents satisfied employees, inflating estimated relationships. Research shows that survey response rates are declining across sectors, raising nonresponse concern.

To address nonresponse error: (1) maximize response rates through incentives, multiple reminders, and convenient timing; (2) compare respondents to non-respondents on available demographics (do they differ?); (3) if possible, conduct a non-response follow-up (contact a sample of non-respondents to understand why they declined).

## Sample Frame Error

Sample frame error occurs when the list from which you sample (the sampling frame) is incomplete or inaccurate. Example: If you sample from "a company's current employee roster" but the roster is outdated and missing recent departures, those departed employees cannot be contacted—under-representing them if they left due to poor psychological safety. Another example: Sampling from "developers on LinkedIn" may under-represent older professionals, small-studio developers, or freelancers outside major cities, systematically missing perspectives.

To mitigate: (1) use the most current and comprehensive frame available; (2) acknowledge frame limitations in reporting; (3) cross-check against known population characteristics.

## Type I Error (False Positive)

Type I error occurs when you reject a true null hypothesis—you conclude an effect exists when it doesn't (a false alarm or false positive). Example: Suppose the true population relationship is that leadership style does NOT predict psychological safety. However, due to sampling variability, your sample happens to show a positive effect, leading you to incorrectly conclude leadership style predicts safety.

Type I error is controlled statistically through significance level (alpha, typically 0.05), meaning you accept a 5% risk of falsely claiming an effect. However, in practice, multiple testing (running many analyses), selective reporting (choosing analyses that "worked"), and publication bias toward surprising findings inflate actual Type I error rates.

To minimize: (1) pre-specify your hypotheses and analyses before data collection; (2) report all conducted tests, not just significant ones; (3) use appropriate corrections for multiple comparisons.

## Type II Error (False Negative / Missed Opportunity)

Type II error occurs when you fail to reject a false null hypothesis—you conclude no effect exists when one actually does (a missed opportunity). Example: Suppose leadership style truly does predict psychological safety in the population, but your sample is too small or noisy to detect the effect statistically, leading you to (incorrectly) conclude no relationship.

Type II error is controlled through statistical power: the probability of detecting a true effect of a given size. Researchers typically aim for 80% power, meaning if an effect truly exists, they have an 80% chance of finding it. Power depends on sample size (larger samples = higher power), effect size (larger effects are easier to detect), and significance level.

To minimize Type II error: (1) conduct power analysis before data collection to ensure adequate sample size; (2) use effect sizes from prior research to calibrate expectations; (3) consider the cost of missing a true effect.

## Bias Mitigation Strategies

(1) **Randomization**: Randomly selecting or assigning participants reduces selection bias, particularly in quantitative research.

(2) **Diverse recruitment methods**: Use multiple channels (email, in-person, posters, referrals) to reach varied participants, not just the most accessible.

(3) **Incentives**: Offer participation incentives (small payment, entry into prize draw, public recognition) to encourage response from otherwise low-motivation groups.

(4) **Non-response follow-up**: Contact a sample of non-respondents to understand why they declined and check if they differ from respondents.

(5) **Sampling frame validation**: Confirm the frame is complete, current, and representative.

(6) **Pre-specification**: Define your hypothesis, variables, and analysis plan before data collection, preventing selective reporting.

(7) **Sensitivity checks**: Analyze data with and without questionable cases to see if conclusions hold.

(8) **Qualitative sampling triangulation**: In qualitative work, use multiple sources or methods (interviews + documents, multiple researchers coding independently) to validate findings.
