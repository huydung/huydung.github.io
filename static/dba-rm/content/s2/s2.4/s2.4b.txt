## Propositions (Qualitative)

Propositions are tentative explanations or predictions about how phenomena relate, framed from a conceptual framework and refined through data collection. Unlike hypotheses, which are fixed before research, propositions evolve as qualitative understanding deepens. They articulate "if-then" or "how" logic: "If leaders demonstrate transparency and inclusivity, then team members will perceive greater psychological safety and be more willing to contribute ideas." Propositions serve as analytical anchors—guiding thematic analysis, organizing findings, and providing logic against which patterns are checked. They are grounded in theory yet remain open to revision, refinement, or replacement as data emerges and understanding develops.

## Tentative Explanations & Predictions
Propositions begin as informed hunches about how things work, derived from literature review, preliminary observation, or theoretical reasoning. They are not rigid predictions but rather starting points for exploration. Initial propositions might be: "Team psychological safety depends primarily on leader credibility," or "Organizational transparency moderates how team members respond to challenging work." These are not hypotheses to test rigidly but tentative explanations to explore and refine.

## Framed from Conceptual Framework
A conceptual framework is the theoretical architecture guiding your research—the key concepts, their relationships, and the logic connecting them. Propositions should align with and emerge from this framework. If your framework integrates leadership theory, organizational structure, and team dynamics, your propositions should reflect these connections: They might predict that hierarchical structures can coexist with team cohesion if leaders demonstrate credibility and clear communication—a culturally informed proposition extending theory.

## Rival Explanations Considered
Strong propositions anticipate alternative explanations and address why one proposition is more likely than another. You might propose: "Team psychological safety depends primarily on leader behavior," yet acknowledge rival explanations: "Perhaps team composition is actually more important, or both factors equally." Considering rivals makes propositions more robust and analysis more critical, preventing tunnel vision and ensuring openness to evidence contradicting initial expectations.

## Logic Model Linking Data to Propositions
Articulate how you will connect data (quotes, themes, patterns) to each proposition. This logic model clarifies the reasoning: "I expect to find evidence of leader credibility in descriptions of how leaders handle mistakes or demonstrate expertise. I expect to find team psychological safety reflected in how openly team members discuss problems." When analyzing data, you then look for this evidence under each proposition, creating an explicit chain from raw data to theoretical claims.

## Emerge & Evolve During Data Collection
Unlike hypotheses, propositions are not fixed before research begins. As you interview participants or analyze documents, patterns emerge that refine, extend, or challenge initial propositions. An initial proposition about leader credibility might evolve to recognize that organizational structures and team composition matter equally. Final propositions represent more nuanced understanding than initial hypotheses could capture—a standard strength of qualitative inquiry.

## "If-Then" Logic
Propositions often follow if-then structure: "If leaders demonstrate transparency and inclusive decision-making, then team members will perceive greater safety and contribute more ideas, *even in hierarchical structures*." This conditional logic articulates mechanisms. Propositions might include: "If organizational decisions are explained clearly and consistently, then team members will accept hierarchy more readily," or "If team members feel their expertise is valued, then they will be more engaged." This reverses some assumptions while maintaining logical coherence.

## Rival Propositions Compared
As data emerges, competing propositions can be evaluated. For example: Proposition A states that "leader credibility is the primary driver of team cohesion." Proposition B states that "organizational structure is the primary driver." As interview data accumulates, you compare which propositions have stronger evidence, leading to refinement or integration. Strong qualitative analysis explicitly compares rival explanations.

## Verification through Pattern Matching
Propositions are evaluated through pattern matching: Do multiple data sources (interviews, observations, documents) support the proposition? Are there rich examples? Do alternative explanations seem less plausible? You verify each proposition by searching your interview transcripts for evidence, counting supporting examples, and considering counterexamples. Strong propositions have multiple confirming instances across different participants; weak propositions have few examples or prominent contradictions, leading to revision or rejection.

## Role in Thematic Analysis
Propositions organize thematic analysis. Instead of creating themes randomly, you create themes aligned with propositions, then look for evidence under each theme. For example, if one proposition is that "leader credibility shapes team cohesion," you'd code interview data for themes related to credibility (competence, trustworthiness, fairness) and team cohesion (trust, collaboration, communication). This structure moves analysis beyond description to explanation, showing how themes support theoretical propositions.
