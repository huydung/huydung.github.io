## Shared Attributes

All strong research questions—whether quantitative or qualitative—share core characteristics that make them investigable and meaningful. These attributes ensure that your question is grounded in reality, worthy of study, and answerable through rigorous research.

## Clear & Unambiguous
The question must be easily understood, with no jargon or vague terms left undefined. Weak: "How do managers affect team stuff?" Strong: "How do supportive leadership behaviors (active listening, inclusive decision-making, and psychological safety) affect team innovation in software development?" Your question should be clear enough that another researcher could understand exactly what is being asked without additional interpretation.

## Specific & Focused
Avoid overreach. A question should not try to answer everything; it should target a bounded phenomenon. Questions should focus on particular relationships or contexts, not sprawl across multiple variables. Specificity makes the research manageable and findings interpretable.

## Researchable & Answerable
Can you actually answer this question through systematic research? Avoid philosophical questions ("What is the meaning of success?") or moral questions ("Should leaders prioritize profit or people?") unless you can operationalize them empirically. Research questions are answerable: Can you measure relevant variables? Can you explore themes through interviews? Researchability distinguishes research questions from casual wondering.

## Identifies Population & Context
Be explicit about who you are studying and where. Vague: "How does leadership work?" Specific: "How does transformational leadership shape team innovation among game developers at mid-sized studios?" Questions should always include the population (who you're studying) and context (where, under what conditions). This specificity increases relevance and allows others to assess generalizability.

## Advances Knowledge & Fills a Gap
Your question should address something not yet well understood. Does it extend existing theory? Challenge an assumption? Study an under-researched group? Your work should advance knowledge by testing whether assumptions hold universally, or by studying populations that have been overlooked, or by applying theories in new contexts.

## Relevant to Stakeholders
Consider who cares about the answer. Is it relevant to practitioners, academics, policymakers, or society? Work is most compelling when it addresses questions that matter to multiple audiences: researchers interested in advancing theory, practitioners seeking to improve effectiveness, and organizations facing real challenges.

## Components: Research Problem + Population + Variables
Every strong RQ contains three elements. The research problem identifies what gap or tension you are addressing. The population specifies who you are studying. The variables (or phenomena) identify what you are measuring or exploring. A strong quantitative RQ: Problem = Does X predict Y contrary to existing assumptions? Population = organizational type Z. Variables = X (predictor), Y (outcome). A qualitative RQ: Problem = How do organizational factors shape experience? Population = team members in context C. Phenomena = leadership influence, team cohesion, communication patterns, etc.

## Focused vs. Unfocused Questions
A focused question addresses one core relationship or phenomenon. "How does transparency in communication affect team trust?" is focused. An unfocused question sprawls: "How do leadership, organizational culture, team dynamics, individual personality, and external market pressures all interact to affect team performance?" While the latter may be interesting, it's not answerable in a single study. Strong RQs are appropriately focused—each targets a specific relationship or set of themes without overextending.
