## Qualitative Data: Nominal & Ordinal

Qualitative data are expressed as language, categories, or descriptions rather than numbers, and are analyzed interpretively to uncover meaning, patterns, and nuance. Qualitative data excel at answering "why" and "how" questions, capturing the complexity of human experience in organizational contexts. Example: Semi-structured interviews with 8 developers asking open-ended questions about their experiences with leadership, trust, and team safety. These interview transcripts are analyzed using thematic coding to identify emerging themes—rich, contextual insights that survey numbers alone could not reveal.

## Nominal Data

Nominal data assign observations to mutually exclusive categories with no inherent order or ranking—gender (male, female, non-binary), department (engineering, design, production), or studio location (Hanoi, Bangkok, San Francisco). Nominal categories represent different types but not magnitude or hierarchy. In organizational research, researchers might categorize team members by role (programmer, artist, designer) or by organizational unit (mobile games, console games, tools) to compare outcomes across groups. Nominal data are analyzed using frequency counts, chi-square tests, or qualitative comparison of category patterns.

## Ordinal Data

Ordinal data assign observations to ranked categories where order matters but the distance between categories is undefined—Likert-scale satisfaction levels (very dissatisfied, dissatisfied, neutral, satisfied, very satisfied), performance ratings (below expectations, meets expectations, exceeds expectations), or seniority levels (junior, mid-level, senior). The categories have a logical sequence, but "satisfied" is not necessarily twice as strong as "neutral." In survey research, ordinal data from interview participant experience levels or from satisfaction scales capture ranked distinctions. Ordinal data are analyzed using medians, percentiles, and non-parametric tests.

## Interpretive Analysis

All qualitative data are ultimately expressed as language and analyzed interpretively to extract meaning, identify patterns, and develop conceptual understanding. Unlike statistical analysis, which reduces data to numbers, interpretive analysis preserves context and complexity. Example: Interview data are coded iteratively—first identifying concrete statements (e.g., "I hesitate to speak up in meetings"), then grouping these into broader themes (e.g., "barriers to voice," "safety concerns," "trust dynamics"). This process transforms raw interview text into meaningful insights about how people experience team dynamics and leadership in their organizational contexts.
