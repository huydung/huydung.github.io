## Quantitative Reporting: Presenting Statistical Findings With Precision

### Conventional Report Structure: From Title to References

Quantitative papers follow predictable structure, allowing readers to navigate efficiently:

**Title**: Specific and precise, indicating study variables and population. "Organizational Culture Predicts Employee Engagement: A Structural Equation Analysis of 150 Professionals" immediately tells readers the topic and approach. Avoid vague titles like "A Study of Engagement."

**Abstract**: 150-250 words synthesizing the entire study. Include: research question, sample size and characteristics, methods, key results, and implications. Readers often decide whether to read based on abstract; make it count.

**Introduction**: Establish context and significance. What problem does this study address? Why should readers care? Trace: What is the focal construct? Why does it matter? What does prior research show? What gap does this study fill? Introduce readers to the conversation, not just the study.

**Methods**: Describe sample, measures, and procedures with sufficient detail for replication. Report: sample size and characteristics; measures (name of scale, items, Cronbach's alpha); procedures (how collected, timeline); data analysis (specific tests, software). Readers should evaluate methodological quality and potentially replicate.

**Results**: Report statistical findings systematically, organized by research question. Typical structure:
- Descriptive Statistics (Table: Means, SDs, correlations)
- Model Fit (Do data support proposed model? Report fit indices)
- Main Findings (Table: Path coefficients with SE, p-values, 95% CI)
- Additional Analyses (Moderation, multi-group comparisons, robustness checks)

In results, report facts: coefficients, p-values, confidence intervals. Interpretation comes in discussion.

**Discussion**: Interpret findings and connect to literature. What do results mean? How do they answer research questions? Are findings consistent with prior work or surprising? What are limitations? Implications for theory and practice? Discussion is where expertise shines; it's analytical and interpretive, not fact-reporting.

**References**: All cited sources, formatted consistently. Accuracy is critical: readers verify citations; errors undermine credibility.

### Statistical Tables and Figures: Precise Presentation

**Descriptive Statistics Table**: Organize variables with mean, standard deviation, range, and inter-item correlations.

| Variable | M | SD | Range | α | 1 | 2 |

| 1. Organizational Culture | 3.8 | 0.7 | 1-5 | 0.78 | — | |
| 2. Employee Engagement | 3.5 | 0.9 | 1-5 | 0.72 | 0.48** | — |

Note: **p<0.01

This table conveys: scale ranges, internal consistency, average scores, and inter-variable correlations at a glance.

**Results Presentation**: Include not just p-values but effect sizes and confidence intervals. Reporting "X predicts Y, p<0.05" is incomplete; better: "X significantly predicts Y (β=0.314, 95% CI [0.154, 0.475], p=0.001)." Effect size conveys magnitude; confidence interval shows plausible range; p-value indicates significance.

**Figures**: Path diagrams (showing hypothesized relationships), plots (showing relationships visually), or graphs illustrating patterns. Figures should be clear, labeled, and stand alone (readable without text).

### Effect Sizes: Moving Beyond P-Values

P-values indicate whether an effect exists but not magnitude. A very large study finds statistically significant tiny effects; a small study might find large effects that miss significance. Effect sizes communicate magnitude. A β=0.314 indicates moderate effect: per unit increase in X, Y increases by about 0.3 units on the scale. This is meaningful—neither trivial nor enormous.

Reporting effect sizes alongside p-values gives complete picture: "X predicts Y (β=0.314, p=0.001)" conveys significance and magnitude, allowing readers to judge practical importance.

### Confidence Intervals: Expressing Uncertainty

A 95% confidence interval around β=0.314 might be [0.154, 0.475], meaning you're 95% confident the true effect lies between these values. Confidence intervals communicate uncertainty: wide intervals [0.05, 0.58] indicate more uncertainty; narrow intervals [0.30, 0.32] indicate precision. Overlapping confidence intervals suggest non-significant differences.

Reporting confidence intervals alongside point estimates is increasingly standard, providing a complete picture of both point estimates and uncertainty.

### Quantitative Writing Conventions

In quantitative sections, write in past tense (active voice where possible): "We analyzed 146 responses using SEM" rather than "Analysis was conducted." Use precise language: "correlated with" (not "related to"), "predicted" (not "affected"), "significant at p<0.05" (not "very significant"). Report results factually in results section; interpretation comes in discussion.
