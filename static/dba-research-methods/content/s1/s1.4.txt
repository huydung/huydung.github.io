# The 6-Step Research Process

Research follows a structured sequence, though the path isn't always linear—researchers often move backward and forward as new questions emerge. Understanding this process helps you organize your own research and evaluate the rigor of others' work.

## Step 1: Identify the Research Problem

The research problem is the gap, puzzle, or practical challenge that motivates your inquiry. A strong research problem is specific, significant, and answerable through empirical investigation. An example: You observe that team effectiveness varies substantially across similar organizations—high in some, lower in others—yet existing research emphasizes universal factors. Your research problem: understand what contextual factors (culture, structure, leadership) enable or hinder effectiveness. This problem is significant (affects organizational performance), specific (bounded to particular contexts), and answerable through research.

## Step 2: Review Existing Literature

Before collecting data, you survey what's already known. A thorough literature review synthesizes theory, identifies gaps, and positions your research within the broader conversation. Systematic review might reveal that most research focused on certain organizational types, overlooking others. Studies might concentrate on individual-level factors while neglecting organizational structure. This review justifies why your study is needed and frames your research questions.

## Step 3: Formulate Hypothesis or Propositions

Based on the literature, you develop specific predictions (quantitative research) or propositions (qualitative research) about what you expect to find. In quantitative research, a hypothesis is testable—"Factor X will predict outcome Y in organizations with structure type Z." In qualitative research, propositions are more exploratory—"Understanding how teams maintain effectiveness requires attention to cultural factors like trust and communication norms." Hypotheses and propositions guide your research design and data collection.

## Step 4: Collect Data

Data collection brings your research from abstract questions to concrete evidence. Methods vary widely: surveys where participants complete questionnaires on key variables, interviews where researchers have conversations to capture perspectives, experiments where some groups receive an intervention while others serve as controls, or observation where researchers document behavior systematically. A scholar-practitioner collects multiple forms of evidence to address different facets of the same problem.

## Step 5: Analyze Data

Analysis transforms raw data into meaningful patterns and conclusions. Quantitative analysis uses statistics to test relationships (computing correlation coefficients or regression estimates). Qualitative analysis identifies themes, narratives, and patterns in text through systematic coding. Mixed-methods analysis integrates both, perhaps explaining why quantitative correlations exist through qualitative narratives about mechanism. Rigorous analysis is transparent: you document your coding rules, statistical decisions, or thematic categorization so others can understand and potentially verify your work.

## Step 6: Conclusions and Recommendations

You synthesize your findings, acknowledge limitations, and discuss implications for theory and practice. Strong conclusions address what the findings mean: Do they confirm expectations? Challenge them? Apply in specific contexts? Recommendations address practical questions: How should practice change based on findings? What should organizations do differently? This final step closes the loop between research and practice, ensuring insights drive decision-making.
