# Paradigms

A research paradigm is a comprehensive worldview—a set of assumptions about reality, knowledge, and how to conduct inquiry. Four major paradigms shape business and social science research, each offering distinct advantages.

## Pragmatism

Pragmatism prioritizes practical solutions over philosophical purity. It accepts multiple interpretations of reality and asks: "What works for this specific problem?" A pragmatist conducting research on team communication might combine quantitative metrics (survey scores) with qualitative interviews (stories of how communication breaks down) because both provide useful insight. Pragmatic research tests whether a proposed solution works in practice across organizational settings, without claiming to reveal absolute truth—just practical evidence of effectiveness. Pragmatism thrives in applied research and organizational settings.

## Positivism

Positivism assumes reality is objective and knowable through observation and measurement. A positivist researcher believes that team dynamics exist independently of anyone's opinion, can be measured with valid instruments, and follow predictable patterns. A quantitative study operating from positivism measures variables on validated scales and tests whether they correlate in mathematically predictable ways. The statistical results claim to represent objective facts about the population studied. Positivism's strength is producing replicable, generalizable findings; its limitation is reducing complex human experience to numbers.

## Realism

Realism acknowledges that reality exists independently of our observations—but our understanding of it is always filtered through perspective and context. Direct realism assumes we perceive reality fairly accurately; critical realism recognizes that our observations and interpretations are shaped by power structures, culture, and history. A critical realist studying team dynamics in different countries would note that how team cohesion is defined and experienced is partly culturally determined, and that what promotes safety in one context might not in another—not because the dynamics are less real, but because cultural context shapes what's real and how it manifests.

## Interpretivism

Interpretivism prioritizes understanding how people create meaning in their world. Rather than measuring objective facts, interpretivists explore lived experience, perspectives, and how social context shapes perception. Research aligned with interpretivist philosophy might conduct interviews to explore how professionals interpret team safety and what factors they see as enabling risk-taking. The themes aren't universal facts but interpretations of how these people understand and experience team dynamics. Interpretivism excels at capturing nuance and context; its limitation is reduced generalizability across groups.
