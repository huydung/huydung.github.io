# Ontology and Epistemology

Ontology and epistemology are foundational philosophical questions that shape how research is designed and interpreted. Together, they define your beliefs about what exists and how you can know it.

## Ontology: What Exists? What is Real?

Ontology asks: What is the nature of reality? Does psychological safety exist as an objective phenomenon independent of observation, or does it only exist in people's subjective experience and interpretation? An objective ontology (like positivism) assumes safety is a measurable phenomenon with consistent features across contexts. A subjective ontology (like interpretivism) argues that safety is socially constructed—understood differently in different cultures and organizations depending on relationships, hierarchy, and individual interpretation. A realist ontology (critical realism) says both are true: safety has real effects (people demonstrably speak up more, take risks, contribute ideas), but how it manifests depends on cultural and organizational context.

## Epistemology: How Do We Know? How is Knowledge Acquired?

Epistemology asks: What counts as valid knowledge, and how do we obtain it? A positivist epistemology says knowledge comes from objective measurement, observation, and statistical analysis—surveys analyzed with sophisticated statistics produce knowledge claims grounded in numbers. An interpretivist epistemology says knowledge comes from understanding people's lived experience and the meanings they create—interview transcripts analyzed for themes produce knowledge about how people interpret phenomena. Neither alone is "complete" knowledge; they provide complementary insights. Pragmatists epistemologically accept multiple knowledge sources: quantitative metrics, qualitative narratives, practical outcomes, and expert judgment all contribute to understanding.

## Combined Ontology and Epistemology

Your ontological and epistemological beliefs determine your research design. If you believe team dynamics are objective (ontology) and that knowledge comes from statistical measurement (epistemology), you'd design a quantitative survey study. If you believe they're interpreted experience (ontology) and that knowledge comes from dialogue and narrative analysis (epistemology), you'd conduct interviews. Mature scholarship recognizes that complex phenomena are simultaneously measurable (quantitative approaches capture real patterns) and contextually interpreted experience (qualitative approaches reveal lived reality), and that understanding them fully requires multiple lenses working together.
