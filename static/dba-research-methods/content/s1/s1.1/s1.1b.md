# Pseudo-Research

Pseudo-research mimics the appearance of research but lacks the rigor, methodology, or integrity that defines genuine inquiry. It can look convincing—surveys, statistics, structured formats—yet fails to meet the standards of objectivity, empirical grounding, or transparency that make research credible. Understanding pseudo-research helps you recognize why some business reports, case studies, or industry claims fall short.

## Lacks Methodology

Pseudo-research doesn't clearly explain how data was collected or why specific methods were chosen. Imagine a consulting report claiming "our survey of leaders shows that team building improves morale" but nowhere stating how many leaders were surveyed, whether the survey was validated, or what "morale" means operationally. Without explicit methodology, readers cannot evaluate the validity of findings. In contrast, rigorous research specifies the intervention design, control groups, measurement timing, and organizational contexts—allowing scrutiny of whether conclusions follow from methods.

## Biased

Biased pseudo-research is shaped by the researcher's predetermined conclusion rather than evidence. A consultant hired by a software company to "prove" that their product increases team productivity may unconsciously guide interviews toward favorable responses or interpret ambiguous data favorably. Rigorous research follows evidence even when findings contradict initial expectations, demonstrating objectivity rather than bias.

## Excludes Contradicting Data

Pseudo-research ignores findings that don't fit the narrative. A team-building company might conduct post-workshop surveys asking "How much did this training help?" (biased question), then publish only the 80% positive responses while ignoring the 20% negative and non-committal answers. Rigorous research acknowledges limitations, counterintuitive findings, and alternative explanations. Well-designed studies identify complexity: outcomes may be influenced by multiple factors working together—not a single dominant cause—because the data reveals multiple perspectives.

## Inconclusive

Pseudo-research makes sweeping claims despite weak evidence. A company might conduct three interviews with employees and conclude "our organizational culture is thriving" without systematically comparing to baseline conditions, measuring other teams, or controlling for confounds. Genuine research qualifies its claims: "In this sample of tech professionals, psychological safety was positively associated with organizational clarity (r = 0.24, p < 0.05)." That precision reflects actual evidence; sweeping claims without such grounding are pseudo-research.
