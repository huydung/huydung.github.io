# Qualitative Coding

Coding is the analytical engine of qualitative research: the systematic labeling and categorizing of data segments to build understanding, condense complexity, and surface patterns. Through iterative cycles of coding—first cycle focusing on granular, descriptive labels, and second cycle synthesizing these into focused categories and theoretical constructs—researchers transform raw data into organized, meaningful knowledge.

## Coding Functions

Coding serves three core functions in qualitative research: deeper reflection on data segments (forcing engagement with nuance and context), condensing raw data into tractable units (reducing hundreds of initial codes to dozens of focused codes), and heuristic familiarity (building the researcher's intimate knowledge of the dataset). Systematic coding forces reflection on each segment's meaning, condenses sprawling narratives into organized categories, and builds deep familiarity with the data that enables insightful interpretation.

## Coding Frames

Codes can be organized in two structural frames: flat frames treat all codes as equally important units, useful for exploratory work where no a priori hierarchy exists; hierarchical frames nest codes within broader categories, useful when theory or early analysis suggests nested relationships. A flat coding approach might treat "voice," "risk," "trust," and "hierarchy" as peer-level concepts; hierarchical coding might nest these under an overarching concept like "psychological safety" as a parent category, with each as a sub-dimension.

## Descriptive Coding

Assign brief labels that summarize the basic topic or subject of a data segment in plain language, without interpretation. Descriptive codes are often nouns or noun phrases ("Team Communication," "Role Clarity," "Manager Support"). These codes are foundational, capturing what the data is literally about. Early descriptive codes might include "Speaking Up in Meetings," "Fear of Judgment," "Team Collaboration," directly reflecting content without analytical transformation.

## In Vivo Coding

Use direct language and phrases from the respondents themselves as codes, honoring their voice and capturing the authenticity of their experience. In vivo codes are often vivid, memorable, and emotionally resonant. If a participant says "We are safe to fail here," that phrase becomes the code—preserving their precise language and emotional weight. Researcher-generated codes like "Permission to Speak," "No Judgment Zone," or "Safe Failure" drawn directly from respondents' words maintain authenticity.

## Process Coding

Label data segments that describe actions, interactions, and processes—often verbs or gerund phrases ("Expressing Ideas," "Resisting Feedback," "Building Trust Over Time"). Process codes capture dynamics and movement, useful when research questions concern how teams evolve or how relationships develop. In longitudinal research, process coding captures codes like "Growing Comfort," "Testing Waters," "Opening Up Gradually," which describe the unfolding of change across time.

## Initial Coding

Create broad, open codes during first contact with data, assigning multiple codes to single segments and allowing overlap and redundancy. Initial coding is exploratory and permissive, erring on the side of over-coding rather than under-coding, because early codes can be refined later. Hundreds of initial codes might emerge from this exploratory phase, where segments receive multiple overlapping codes (e.g., a segment about team interaction might be coded as "Leadership," "Trust-Building," "Modeling Vulnerability," and "Cultural Influence").

## Emotion Coding

Label segments that convey emotions, moods, or affective states—fear, trust, frustration, pride, anxiety. Emotion coding is particularly valuable in studies of interpersonal phenomena like organizational safety, where affect is central to the experience. When an interviewee describes nervousness before speaking in a meeting or relief after their idea is welcomed, emotion codes ("Anxiety," "Relief," "Belonging") capture the affective dimension that shapes behavior.

## Values Coding

Identify segments that express values, beliefs, priorities, or ideologies held by participants. Values codes reveal what people care about—e.g., "Innovation," "Loyalty," "Fairness," "Continuous Learning." In organizational contexts, values coding might surface codes like "Collaboration," "Accountability," or "Psychological Well-Being," showing what team members prioritize and what principles guide their behavior.

## Pattern Coding

In the second coding cycle, identify clusters of first-cycle codes that share commonalities or form larger explanatory patterns. Pattern codes are more abstract and interpretive than first-cycle codes, synthesizing multiple related codes into meta-categories. If first-cycle codes include "Speaking Up," "Sharing Ideas," "Voicing Concerns," and "Contributing to Decisions," a pattern code might be "Agency and Voice," consolidating these into a larger concept.

## Focused Coding

Synthesize the most frequent, significant, or analytically relevant first-cycle codes into focused categories that form the backbone of thematic analysis. Focused codes are refined, efficient, and theoretically grounded. After identifying "Agency and Voice" as a pattern, focused coding might produce "Organizational Voice"—a stable, well-defined analytical unit that represents a core aspect of the phenomenon.

## Axial Coding

In structured second-cycle approaches, organize codes around axes or dimensions—examining conditions, actions/interactions, and consequences. Axial coding asks: Under what conditions does this phenomenon occur? What actions/interactions follow? What are the consequences? In organizational research, axial coding might organize codes around axes like "Contextual Conditions" (e.g., team size, leadership style), "Culture-Building Actions" (e.g., modeling vulnerability, responding non-defensively), and "Consequences" (e.g., increased contribution, improved outcomes).

## Theoretical Coding

Propose higher-order theoretical constructs that explain relationships among focused codes and integrate them into a coherent conceptual model. Theoretical codes are interpretive and explanatory, often drawing on existing theory or synthesizing new theory from the data. Final integrated themes represent theoretical codes—conceptual units that explain the phenomenon's origins, manifestations, and impacts.

## Jotting and Analytic Memoing

Write brief notes ("jottings") during data collection and formal analytical memos between coding cycles. Jottings capture immediate impressions, questions, and observations; analytic memos develop these into sustained analytical writing, exploring code meanings, patterns, relationships, and emerging themes. Memoing creates an audit trail of analytical thinking and often surfaces unexpected connections or contradictions.

## Within-Case vs. Cross-Case Analysis

Within-case analysis examines patterns, themes, and relationships within a single case (e.g., one team, one organization), honoring its unique context and configuration. Cross-case analysis identifies patterns and themes that emerge consistently across multiple cases, enabling more generalizable insights. Both levels enrich understanding: within-case analysis preserves nuance; cross-case analysis identifies patterns transcending individual contexts.
