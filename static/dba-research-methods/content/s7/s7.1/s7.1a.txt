# Quantitative 9-Step Process

Structured quantitative analysis progresses systematically from conceptual clarity through statistical testing to synthesized results. Each step builds on the previous, ensuring that assumptions are met, hypotheses are properly specified, and interpretations are defensible. This framework has been applied across research domains—from tech industry studies examining team dynamics to educational research on organizational change.

## Step 1: Clarify Problem Hierarchy

Organize research questions and hypotheses into a logical hierarchy that shows relationships and dependencies. This means identifying primary questions, secondary sub-questions, and the theoretical logic linking them. Clear hierarchy prevents muddled analysis and keeps teams aligned on priorities. For example, a primary question might ask whether organizational culture influences team cohesion, with secondary questions probing how specific cultural norms like psychological safety or trust mediate that relationship.

## Step 2: Define Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Specify H₀ (null hypothesis) and Hₐ (alternative hypothesis) with precision before analyzing data. H₀ typically asserts no relationship; Hₐ asserts the relationship hypothesized in theory. This formal specification prevents p-hacking and ensures statistical tests are interpretable. In a study of team dynamics, H₀ might state that organizational hierarchy has no direct effect on employee engagement; Hₐ predicts a significant effect mediated through psychological safety.

## Step 3: Examine Raw Data

Before any transformation or test, visually inspect the raw dataset for anomalies, missing values, outliers, and data entry errors. Spreadsheet inspection, descriptive summaries, and early scatterplots reveal patterns that guide cleaning decisions. In a survey of workplace culture across multiple teams, examining raw responses might reveal that one respondent selected "strongly agree" for every item—a red flag for inattentive responding that could skew results.

## Step 4: Data Entry and Cleaning (Excel/SPSS/R)

Systematically code responses, verify entries against source documents, and flag or correct errors. Tools like Excel, SPSS, and R Commander provide structured environments for this task. When entering survey data on team psychological safety from 150 respondents, verification of item ranges (e.g., 1–5 scales) and flagging out-of-range values ensures data quality before analysis begins.

## Step 5: Distribution Check

Test whether variables meet assumptions for planned statistical tests, particularly normality. Histograms, Q-Q plots, and formal tests (Shapiro–Wilk, Anderson–Darling) assess departures from normality. If variables are severely non-normal, transformations or non-parametric alternatives may be required. In a survey on team stress across game development studios, skewed distributions in burnout scores might warrant Spearman correlation instead of Pearson.

## Step 6: Redefine Variables if Needed

If assumptions are violated, consider transformations (log, square-root, Box–Cox), recoding, or compositing items into indices. Composite scales often improve normality and reduce measurement error. Computing multi-item measures from raw survey responses—combining individual items into indices for constructs like organizational trust or team cohesion—improves distributional properties and conceptual clarity.

## Step 7: Test Hypotheses

Conduct planned statistical tests (t-tests, ANOVA, correlation, regression, or SEM) and report point estimates, confidence intervals, and p-values. Document all tests, including exploratory analyses. A mediation analysis might test both direct and indirect paths, examining whether organizational culture predicts employee outcomes directly or through the mechanism of psychological safety.

## Step 8: Control Variables and Covariates

Include variables that rival explanations (e.g., tenure, role, department) in the model to isolate focal relationships. Ancova or regression approaches can adjust for covariates. In a study of team psychological safety in a game studio, including department or team size as covariates might control for structural differences that confound relationships between culture and outcomes.

## Step 9: Revisit Research Questions and Draft Results

Return to the original research questions and synthesize findings into a coherent narrative. Articulate what hypotheses were supported, effect sizes, confidence intervals, and limitations. Findings should directly address the original research aims, explaining not just what was found but what it means in context.
