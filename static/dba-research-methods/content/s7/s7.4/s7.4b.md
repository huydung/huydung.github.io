# Qualitative Data Display

Qualitative displays organize codes, themes, narratives, and relationships into visual or tabular forms that reveal patterns and facilitate interpretation. Rather than reducing data to numbers, qualitative displays preserve narrative richness while imposing analytical structure. Matrices arrange coded segments by code and case; network diagrams show relationships and causal chains; flowcharts sequence events or decisions. These displays transform the researcher's mental models into shareable, verifiable artifacts that readers can follow and potentially challenge.

## Matrix Displays

Tabular arrays with rows and columns representing cases, participants, codes, or themes, with cells containing evidence, frequencies, or analytical notes. Matrices compress and organize large volumes of qualitative data, revealing patterns and gaps. A code-by-case matrix shows which codes appear in which cases, revealing which themes are universal versus case-specific. A theme-by-theme matrix shows relationships among themes, revealing hierarchies or dependencies. In qualitative analysis, a matrix might display 7 final themes as columns, with interview segments as rows, each cell containing relevant coded excerpt—making evidence transparent and allowing readers to verify theme validity.

## Network Displays

Nodes represent codes, themes, or constructs; lines represent relationships, causal links, or correlations. Network diagrams visualize complex relationships that would be difficult to describe in prose. In organizational research, a network diagram might show "Leadership Practices" and "Team Safety" as nodes connected by an arrow (causal), with "Performance" as a downstream node; additional edges might represent feedback loops or conditional relationships. Thematic analysis could produce a network diagram showing final themes as interconnected nodes, with arrows indicating how themes reinforce or influence each other.

## Role-Ordered Matrices

Organize data by participant role or position, revealing whether patterns differ by social location. A role-ordered matrix might display codes or themes in rows, with columns for each role (manager, individual contributor, designer, engineer), showing whether phenomena operate differently across roles. In organizational studies, a role-ordered matrix might reveal that "Voice and Agency" is prominent for individual contributors but that safety emerges differently among managers (who frame it as responsibility for creating it) versus frontline employees (who frame it as permission to contribute).

## Time-Ordered Matrices

Arrange data chronologically or developmentally, revealing processes and trajectories. A time-ordered matrix might display interview segments on the y-axis and time periods (Month 1, Month 3, Month 6, Month 12) on the x-axis, showing how themes or codes evolve. In longitudinal research, a time-ordered matrix might track pre-intervention, mid-intervention, and post-intervention data, showing how participant experience, team dynamics, and relationship quality evolved across the study period.

## Event Listings

Chronological narrative tables cataloging critical events, decisions, or turning points relevant to the research question, with columns for date, event description, participants, and analytical notes. Event listings preserve narrative flow while enabling comparative analysis. In organizational research, an event listing might chronicle moments when trust was built (team member spoke up and was affirmed; manager modeled vulnerability) or broken (critical comment; decision made without input), illuminating conditions that foster or undermine safety.

## Flowcharts

Diagrams showing sequences of decisions, actions, or conditions using boxes, diamonds, and arrows. Flowcharts are particularly useful for displaying processes—how teams respond to challenges, how leaders build safety, how ideas flow from conception to implementation. In organizational research, a flowchart might depict the decision point: "Team member has an idea" → "Does team feel psychologically safe?" → Yes: Speak up, contribute; No: Withhold idea, risking missed innovation.

## Causal Networks and Causal Chains

Visual models showing causal relationships and sequences. A causal chain displays linear sequences (X → Y → Z); a causal network shows multiple paths and feedback loops. In organizational research, a causal network might depict: Leadership modeling vulnerability (condition) → Team perceives safety (mechanism) → Team members contribute ideas (behavior) → Team performance improves (outcome) → Leader reinforces safety through celebration (feedback loop). Qualitative analysis likely produces evidence of such causal chains, demonstrating how phenomena emerge from specific conditions and generate downstream effects.
