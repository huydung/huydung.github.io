## Search & Evaluation

Finding relevant literature requires strategic searching across multiple sources, combined with critical evaluation of source credibility and quality. A good search uses diverse platforms, multiple keywords, and chain-of-references methods to avoid missing important work. Evaluation requires assessing author credibility, methodological rigor, and relevance to your RQ.

## Sources: Google Scholar
Google Scholar indexes peer-reviewed articles, preprints, books, and dissertations across disciplines. It is freely accessible and comprehensive, though it includes some non-peer-reviewed content. Use Google Scholar with focused keywords like "organizational transparency team innovation," or "hierarchical leadership effectiveness" to identify relevant literature. Strengths: comprehensive, free, good for discovering seminal papers. Weaknesses: includes non-peer-reviewed content, search results not always ranked by quality.

## Sources: Academic Libraries & Databases
University libraries provide access to peer-reviewed databases like JSTOR, ProQuest, EBSCO, and subject-specific databases (PsycINFO for psychology, Business Source Complete for management). These databases offer more rigorous filtering and advanced search features. Search these platforms using controlled vocabulary and peer-review filters to ensure quality.

## Sources: Peer-Reviewed Journals
Prioritize peer-reviewed journal articles over other sources. Peer review signals that work has been vetted by expert reviewers and meets disciplinary standards. Identify key journals in your field and search within them to ensure quality and relevance.

## Sources: Government & Institutional Publications
Government agencies, think tanks, and research institutes publish reports, white papers, and data that may be relevant. Industry reports, labor statistics, and organizational development publications provide context and practical grounding. Evaluate these critically—some are advocacy-oriented rather than purely empirical.

## Author Credibility: Degree & Specialization
Assess author expertise by examining credentials, institutional affiliation, and specialization. Is the author a professor in a relevant field? Does their publication history show sustained engagement with your topic? High-credibility authors have relevant terminal degrees, senior academic positions, and extensive publication records in the field.

## Author Credibility: Publication History & Citations
How much has this author published? How widely cited are their works? Authors with many publications on a topic demonstrate sustained expertise. Citation counts show how influential their work is—highly cited papers have shaped the field. Note: newer authors with fewer citations are not automatically less credible (particularly in emerging fields), but established scholars with extensive citations have proven impact.

## Filtering by Citation Count
Start with highly cited sources to understand dominant perspectives and foundational concepts. Then work backward (through references) and forward (through citing papers) to discover nuances, contradictions, and contemporary developments. Citation count is one filter among several—not all important work is highly cited.

## References Chain Method
Once you find a key source, examine its reference list to discover related work (backward chaining) and search Google Scholar for papers citing it (forward chaining). This method ensures comprehensive coverage and reveals how scholarship has evolved. It also reveals when a paper contradicts or extends prior work.

## Publication Date & Chronological Order
Recent sources show current thinking; older sources provide foundational concepts and historical context. A good review spans both. Include classic foundational papers alongside contemporary studies to show evolution of thinking and where recent challenges to established theory emerge.

## Evaluation: Author Advocacy & Bias
Consider the author's perspective and potential biases. Is the author advocating for a particular viewpoint? Are they affiliated with an organization that has a stake in conclusions? This context informs interpretation—the research may be credible but shaped by applied interests. Evaluating advocacy helps you read critically.

## Evaluation: Alternate Variables & Competing Explanations
Does the source acknowledge alternate explanations for its findings? A source that only presents evidence supporting its conclusion is weaker than one that thoughtfully considers alternatives. Value sources that acknowledge: "While this factor might drive outcomes in certain contexts, organizational factors or leadership approaches might matter differently in other contexts."

## Evaluation: Chronological Evolution of Concepts
Understand how concepts have evolved over time. Early research might emphasize individual traits; contemporary research emphasizes team dynamics or cultural context. Tracing evolution prevents outdated thinking and reveals where new knowledge is emerging.
