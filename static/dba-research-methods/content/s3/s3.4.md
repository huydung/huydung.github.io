## Structure & Drafting

A literature review follows a clear architecture: Introduction (defining scope and RQ), Body (organizing and synthesizing sources), and Conclusion (summarizing contributions and identifying gaps). The body can be organized through several structures, each suited to different review purposes. A strong literature review moves readers from understanding foundational concepts through contemporary research to identifying specific gaps—creating logical flow and persuasive coherence.

## Three-Part Structure: Introduction → Body → Conclusion

### Introduction
The introduction orients readers to the review topic and establishes its scope. It defines key terms, explains why the topic matters, and presents the organizing question or thesis. Example: "Team innovation has been identified as critical for organizational competitiveness. However, research on innovation is dominated by Western organizational contexts. This review examines whether Western assumptions about factors that enable or inhibit innovation hold across different organizational types and cultural contexts."

### Body
The body synthesizes sources, identifies patterns and contradictions, and builds argument. It moves systematically through key concepts, theories, and empirical findings, showing relationships between sources. The body is organized around themes or topics, not individual sources.

### Conclusion
The conclusion summarizes what the review revealed: What is well-established? Where are contradictions? What gaps remain? How does the research inform future investigation? Example: "While team innovation research has established its importance for organizational performance, it remains concentrated in certain industries and organizational types. Emerging evidence suggests that hierarchical structures and organizational constraints may relate differently to innovation than Western theory predicts. Quantitative research in under-studied organizational contexts and qualitative exploration of mechanisms are needed."

## Body Structures: Swiss Cheese Model
Organize the review around central themes, with each theme represented by multiple sources (some "holes" may be unexplored, representing gaps). Example structure:

**Team Innovation Concept**: What is innovation? How is it measured? What does research show about its importance? (foundational work + contemporary refinements)

**Organizational Structure & Innovation**: What does research show about how hierarchy affects innovation? What contradictions exist? (studies from different organizational types)

**Leadership & Innovation**: How do leaders enable or inhibit team innovation? What evidence exists? (leadership research + team dynamics)

**Cultural Context & Innovation**: How might national or organizational culture shape innovation relationships? (cross-cultural research + contextual studies)

**Contradiction & Gap**: What does Western theory predict? What do context-specific observations suggest? Where is the empirical gap? (critical synthesis)

This structure reveals both knowledge and gaps, showing readers why investigation is necessary.

## Body Structures: Topical Order
Organize around key topics, moving from foundational (simple, broad) to complex (specific, nuanced):

1. **Innovation Fundamentals**: Define the construct, show why it matters, establish measurement approaches.
2. **Leadership & Innovation**: How do leader behaviors shape innovation? What does research show?
3. **Organizational Structure & Innovation**: How do organizational norms and structures influence innovation?
4. **Cultural Dimensions & Innovation**: How might organizational context moderate relationships?
5. **Emerging Research on Context-Specific Dynamics**: What does research suggest about context-dependent effects?
6. **The Gap**: Why does investigation in under-studied contexts matter?

This structure moves readers from simple to complex, building understanding cumulatively.

## Body Structures: Inverted Pyramid
Start with your specific research gap and zoom out, showing how broader contexts and theories relate to it. This foregrounds your contribution:

1. **The Gap**: Under-studied organizational contexts lack research on specific relationships.
2. **Why It Matters**: These contexts are growing in importance; their dynamics may differ from studied populations.
3. **What Theory Predicts**: Dominant theory makes certain predictions about relationships.
4. **What Contradictions Suggest**: Emerging evidence hints at different dynamics in certain contexts.
5. **What We Need to Know**: Empirical evidence from new populations; understanding of mechanisms.
6. **How Your Research Addresses It**: Your investigation in the identified gap.

This structure prioritizes your research, making its significance clear immediately.

## Scope: Key Theories & Current Research
Determine what to include and exclude. A review on team innovation might include:

**Include**: Innovation theory (foundational definitions and contemporary developments); leadership research; organizational structure studies; studies of relevant industries or organizational types.

**Exclude**: General motivation theory (unless directly relevant); HR practices unrelated to innovation; research on unrelated topics.

Scope decisions keep the review focused and manageable.

## Scope: Viewpoints & Expert Conversation
Show that you understand the scholarly conversation. Include multiple perspectives: different theoretical traditions (Western organizational psychology, cross-cultural management, contextual approaches), different methodologies (quantitative, qualitative, mixed), different geographic origins (Western and non-Western research). This breadth signals sophisticated understanding.

## Critical Discussion: Subtopic Categories Connected to RQ
Organize the body around subtopics that directly address your RQ. Each subtopic should: (1) explain a concept, (2) present what research shows, (3) identify gaps or contradictions, (4) explicitly connect back to your RQ.

Example subtopic structure:
**Subtopic: How Organizational Structure Relates to Innovation**
- What is organizational structure? (conceptual background)
- What do Western studies predict about its effects on innovation? (dominant perspective)
- What evidence exists? (research findings)
- What contradictions emerge? (conflicting results or context-specific observations)
- How does this connect to your RQ? (The connection to your investigation)

Each subtopic becomes a building block in the argument for your research.

## Critical Discussion: Synthesis vs. Summary
Move beyond summarizing individual sources to synthesizing across them. Weak: "Author A found X. Author B found Y." Strong: "While Authors A and B both found that X relates to Y, Author C's contradictory finding suggests that moderating variables matter. This contradiction is critical to understanding whether effects are universal or require context."

Synthesis demonstrates critical thinking and positions your research as resolving contradictions.

## Common Mistakes: Poor Organization & Structure
Weak: Arranging sources chronologically with no thematic coherence. Strong: Organizing around themes with chronological depth. ("Innovation research has evolved from early work emphasizing individual traits, through contemporary research examining team dynamics and organizational factors, to emerging research exploring cultural contingency...")

## Common Mistakes: Citation Errors & Weak Documentation
Misrepresenting sources or providing incomplete citations undermines credibility. Always: directly access sources; quote or paraphrase accurately; provide complete citations. Every claim should be traceable to a specific source.

## Common Mistakes: No Synthesis or Critical Analysis
Listing sources without analyzing interrelationships or implications. A strong review doesn't just describe sources; it analyzes patterns, contradictions, and what they mean. Synthesize to reveal: dominant perspectives, gaps, contradictions, and implications for your research.
