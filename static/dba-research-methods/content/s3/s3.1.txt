## Fundamentals & Aims

A literature review is built on three foundational principles: critical analysis (not mere summary), credible sources (peer-reviewed, scholarly, authoritative), and multiple perspectives (representing different viewpoints, methods, and contexts). These principles ensure that the review is rigorous, balanced, and trustworthy. The literature review serves seven distinct purposes, each contributing to research quality and significance. Strong reviews accomplish all seven: identify gaps in existing research, reveal contradictions between theories or findings, justify chosen research methodology, establish the researcher's credibility, contextualize the work within broader scholarly conversation, demonstrate the necessity of investigation, and articulate what the research will contribute.

## Critical Analysis, Not Summary
A weak literature review merely summarizes what each source says: "Author A found X; Author B found Y; Author C concluded Z." A strong literature review analyzes and synthesizes: "While Author A and Author B both found positive effects of X on Y, Author C's contradictory finding suggests that contextual factors (such as organizational size or industry) may moderate this relationship. This contradiction points to a gap in cross-cultural research." Analysis moves beyond description to interpretation, showing relationships between sources and what patterns mean.

## Credible Sources
Base your review on authoritative, scholarly sources: peer-reviewed journal articles, books by established scholars, government reports, and institutional research. Avoid relying on blogs, opinion pieces, or unvetted websites. Strong reviews draw on foundational research in their field, contemporary studies, and credible reports that provide context and evidence. This credibility foundation strengthens your authority to make claims about what is known.

## Multiple Perspectives
Seek out diverse viewpoints, methods, and contexts. If all your sources show similar findings, you may be experiencing confirmation bias or working in an echo chamber. A strong review includes: conflicting findings (and explains why they might differ), different methodologies (quantitative, qualitative, mixed), and various contexts. This breadth prevents oversimplified understanding and reveals nuance.

## Aim 1: Find Gaps
Identify what is *not* yet known. What populations have been under-studied? What contexts under-researched? What relationships untested? Examples: psychological safety research dominates Western samples; leadership effectiveness studies focus on certain industries; game development contexts are under-represented in organizational psychology. These gaps justify your investigation.

## Aim 2: Identify Relationships & Contradictions
Map connections between existing studies and note when findings diverge. Do multiple studies agree on a relationship? Do some findings contradict others? Examples: Western studies consistently predict that hierarchical structures inhibit innovation; yet case studies from high-hierarchy organizations sometimes show strong innovation. This contradiction becomes your research problem.

## Aim 3: Contextualize Your Work
Show how your research fits into the broader scholarly conversation. Is it an extension of existing work? A replication in a new context? A challenge to prevailing assumptions? Contextualization answers: How does my investigation build on what scholars already know?

## Aim 4: Establish Your Credibility
Demonstrate that you understand the field, know the key scholars and theories, and can speak authoritatively about the state of knowledge. A thorough, critical literature review signals competence and rigor. This credibility enables readers to trust your subsequent research.

## Aim 5: Justify Your Methodology
Explain why your chosen research methods are appropriate given the state of knowledge and the research question. A mixed-methods design might be justified because: "Quantitative research tests specific relationships in a large sample, while qualitative research explores mechanisms. Together, they provide both breadth and depth."

## Aim 6: Demonstrate Necessity
Persuade readers that your research is needed—that gaps exist, contradictions demand resolution, or practical problems require investigation. Without this, even well-executed research seems unimportant. Demonstrate necessity by showing consequences: If this gap is not addressed, what is the cost to theory or practice?

## Aim 7: Articulate Contribution
Explain what your research will add to the field. How will it advance theory? Improve practice? Inform policy? An intervention study might contribute by showing whether a proposed solution works; a qualitative study might contribute by revealing how people experience phenomena; a quantitative study might contribute by testing theoretical predictions in new populations.
