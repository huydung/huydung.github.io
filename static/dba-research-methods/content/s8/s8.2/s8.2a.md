## Quantitative Reliability: Consistency of Measurement

### Stability: Do measures yield consistent results over time?

Test-retest reliability measures whether an instrument produces the same results when administered to the same people at different times (assuming the construct hasn't truly changed). If you administer a scale to a group on Monday and Wednesday, and conditions haven't shifted, scores should be very similar. In longitudinal studies, collecting baseline and follow-up data allows assessment of stability: if control group scores remain relatively consistent during a no-intervention period, score fluctuations in an intervention group can more confidently be attributed to treatment effects rather than measurement instability.

### Equality: Do all items on a scale measure the construct equally well?

Item-level consistency asks whether each survey item contributes equally to measuring the construct. A scale with five items should have all five items correlating with the overall score; if one item has very low correlation, it may be ambiguous or measuring something different. Cronbach's alpha calculation directly tests this: alpha measures inter-item correlations, confirming that items hang together as a coherent measure rather than measuring disparate constructs.

### Internal Consistency: Do multiple items measuring the same construct correlate highly?

Cronbach's alpha (α) quantifies internal consistency: α ≥ 0.90 is often considered excellent, 0.80–0.89 good, 0.70–0.79 acceptable, and below 0.70 questionable. Alpha values ≥0.70 indicate that the multiple items on a scale are consistently measuring the same construct rather than random noise. This assurance means that combining multiple items provides a reliable composite measure of the underlying construct.

### Interrater Reliability: Do independent raters agree on measurement?

In studies where human judgment assigns scores (e.g., coding interview data, rating video recordings), interrater reliability measures whether multiple independent raters assign the same or similar scores. Cohen's kappa (κ) or Krippendorff's alpha quantify agreement. If multiple coders independently analyze the same interview transcript and both identify similar themes, their agreement strengthens reliability claims that theme identification isn't idiosyncratic to one coder's interpretation.

### Key Principle: Reliability = Consistency of Measurement

Across all these forms, reliability measures consistency. A reliable measure produces similar scores for the same person at different times, on different items measuring the same construct, and by different raters evaluating the same data. This consistency is prerequisite for drawing valid conclusions: if measurement is wildly inconsistent, findings derived from it cannot be trusted.
