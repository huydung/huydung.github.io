## Quantitative Validity: Ensuring Measurement Accuracy

### Content Validity: Do items represent the construct domain?

Content validity verifies that survey items adequately sample the full range of the construct being measured. For organizational safety, content validity requires items covering multiple dimensions—feeling safe to speak up, interpersonal trust, risk tolerance, voice behavior. Using established scales with strong prior validation ensures item coverage includes the full range of construct dimensions relevant to the research question.

### Criterion Validity: Do measures predict or correlate with outcomes?

Criterion validity has two forms: predictive (does measure X predict future outcome Y?) and concurrent (does measure X correlate with measure Y at the same time?). In organizational research, criterion validity is demonstrated when a cultural measure (measured via an established scale) shows significant relationship with predicted outcomes (e.g., engagement, performance), confirming that the measure validly captures the cultural construct influencing those outcomes.

### Convergent and Discriminant Validity: Do measures of the same construct correlate, and different constructs diverge?

Convergent validity tests whether multiple indicators of one construct correlate highly (e.g., several items measuring safety should intercorrelate). Discriminant validity ensures that measures of different constructs don't correlate so highly they're measuring the same thing. SEM analysis tests both: convergent validity confirms that items cluster together (high loadings on the latent variable), while discriminant validity ensures different constructs load on separate factors, proving they measure distinct phenomena.

### Common Validity Threats and Mitigation

**History & Maturation**: External events or natural change over time threaten validity. Experimental designs using control groups allow separation of intervention effects from historical events or maturation effects.

**Measurement Error**: Imprecise measurement introduces noise. Mitigation: use standardized instruments rather than novel instruments, and conduct pilot testing to refine items before main data collection.

**Regression to the Mean**: Extreme scores tend to become more average on retest. Mitigation: use comparison groups without intervention; if control groups also regress, regression is distinguished from treatment effects.

**Attrition**: Participants dropping out bias results if dropouts differ systematically from remainers. Mitigation: track attrition and test whether dropouts differ on key demographics; intention-to-treat analysis includes all initially recruited participants.

**Environmental Threats**: Changes in setting (new management, reorganization, external events) confound findings. Mitigation: standardize measurement conditions and use multiple sites to assess whether results hold across environmental variation.
