## Shared Concepts Across Methods

### Internal Validity: Does the study measure what it intends?

Internal validity asks whether your research design accurately captures the relationship or effect you claim to observe. In experimental designs, it's about causal inference: did the treatment actually cause the observed outcome? Experimental studies employ random assignment to strengthen internal validity—participants are randomly assigned to intervention or control conditions, reducing selection bias. The pre/post-test design allows measurement of change before and after an intervention, clarifying whether a treatment truly influenced outcomes rather than pre-existing differences.

### External Validity: Can findings generalize beyond the study context?

External validity concerns whether your results apply beyond your specific sample and setting to other populations, times, and contexts. A study with high internal validity but low external validity finds a true effect in one narrow context that may not replicate elsewhere. Research in organizational settings raises questions about generalization to different organizational types or cultures; however, studies that include diverse participant types or multiple sites enable some cross-context generalizability testing.

### Construct Validity: Are constructs accurately operationalized?

Construct validity ensures that your operational measures (survey items, interview questions, codes) truly reflect the theoretical constructs you claim to study. For instance, does a culture scale actually measure how organizations value and enact certain norms? Using established, previously validated instruments lends construct validity through prior validation. In qualitative work, triangulation—combining interview data with observational evidence and member checking with participants—ensures that emergent themes genuinely capture participants' experiences rather than researcher interpretation artifacts.
