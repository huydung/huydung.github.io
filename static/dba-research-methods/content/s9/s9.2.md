## Research Misconduct: Understanding and Preventing Integrity Violations

Research misconduct—fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism—represents fundamental violations of research integrity. These are not matters of judgment or interpretation but clear violations. The research community's credibility depends on identifying and addressing misconduct firmly.

### Plagiarism: Using others' work without credit

Plagiarism means presenting someone else's intellectual work (words, ideas, data, analysis) as your own without attribution. It violates intellectual property rights and misleads readers about idea originality.

**Verbatim Plagiarism**: Copying text directly without quotation marks or citation. If someone copies a paragraph from an organizational psychology paper into their own work without quotation marks and citation, that's plagiarism, even if the paper is later cited in references.

**Paraphrasing Without Citation**: Restating others' ideas in your own words but without attribution is still plagiarism. Rewriting "Culture shapes team dynamics" as "In teams, cultural norms influence how members interact" without citing the original source constitutes plagiarism of the concept.

**Idea Plagiarism**: Presenting others' theoretical frameworks or research designs as novel. Adapting an established theoretical model wholesale without citing the original represents plagiarism of the framework.

**Self-Plagiarism**: Reusing your own previous work (papers, data, analysis) without disclosure or permission. Publishing the same analysis in multiple journals without disclosing prior publication violates exclusive publication agreements and constitutes research misconduct.

**Mitigation**: Careful attribution of all sources—citing original authors whenever using their words, ideas, theories, or data; clear quotation marks around direct quotes; paraphrase with citation for ideas; disclosure when reusing previous work.

### Fabrication: Creating false data

Fabrication means inventing data that weren't actually collected—manufacturing results from thin air. If someone invented interview quotes that were never actually said, or made up survey responses that never happened, that's fabrication.

**Why It Occurs**: Pressure to publish positive findings, desire to complete work quickly, or discouragement when data don't support hypotheses can tempt researchers toward invention.

**Why It's Serious**: Fabricated data corrupts the scientific record. Subsequent researchers citing fabricated findings waste resources conducting follow-up studies based on false conclusions. In practice, fabricated evidence that an intervention "works" might lead organizations to invest in ineffective programs; other researchers might replicate programs expecting results that don't materialize.

**Mitigation**: Collect and report data honestly; if data don't support hypotheses, report honestly—null findings and unexpected patterns are scientifically valuable; implement data audits where supervisors randomly verify that collected data (recordings, responses) match reported findings.

### Falsification: Manipulating data to fit hypotheses

Falsification means altering, omitting, or selectively reporting data to make findings appear more favorable. Unlike fabrication (inventing data), falsification manipulates existing data.

**Selective Reporting**: Reporting only analyses that support your hypothesis while omitting analyses that contradict it. Running multiple regression analyses and reporting only those yielding significant results while omitting non-significant analyses constitutes falsification through selective reporting.

**Outlier Removal**: Excluding data points that don't fit the expected pattern under the guise of "error correction." Falsely labeling a response as measurement error and removing it because it contradicts your hypothesis is falsification.

**P-hacking**: Running multiple statistical tests and reporting only those yielding p<0.05, creating false positive findings. Testing 20 different relationships and reporting the 3 significant ones while omitting the 17 non-significant ones falsifies the true effect.

**HARKing** (Hypothesizing After Results are Known): Presenting post-hoc findings as if they were pre-specified hypotheses. Finding an unexpected relationship in data then writing the hypothesis section claiming you predicted it is falsification.

**Mitigation**: Pre-register hypotheses and analysis plans before data analysis; report all analyses regardless of significance; justify exclusion criteria before analyzing data; conduct robustness checks ensuring findings hold across reasonable analytical choices; have independent analysts reproduce results.

### Citation Integrity: Accurate representation of sources

Citation integrity extends ethical obligation to properly represent what sources actually say:

**Misquoting**: Altering a quotation to change its meaning. If an author wrote "Hierarchy can sometimes support efficiency but often inhibits innovation," citing this as "Hierarchy inhibits innovation" misrepresents the original nuance.

**Out-of-Context Citation**: Quoting accurately but removing context that changes meaning. Citing "Hierarchies improve safety" from a sentence that originally stated "In crisis situations, hierarchies improve safety but in normal operations they inhibit safety" strips essential context.

**Non-existent Citation**: Citing a paper you haven't actually read and verified. Researchers should cite only sources they've directly reviewed; citing secondhand references without reading the original risks misrepresenting findings.

**Mitigation**: Quote sparingly and only from sources you've directly read; provide context around quotations; verify all citations before submission; use citation management software reducing transcription errors.
