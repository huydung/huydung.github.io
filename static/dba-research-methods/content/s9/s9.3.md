## IRB & Informed Consent: Protecting Human Research Subjects

### IRB: The Institutional Review Board's Protective Role

An Institutional Review Board (IRB) is an independent committee that reviews research involving human subjects to ensure ethical conduct and regulatory compliance. Every institution conducting human research maintains an IRB. The IRB evaluates proposed research before it begins, focusing on: (1) risks to participants, (2) adequacy of informed consent, (3) fair subject selection, and (4) risk-benefit ratio (do potential benefits justify risks to subjects?).

Qualitative and experimental research involving semi-structured interviews or behavioral interventions requires IRB approval before participants are recruited. The IRB reviews protocols examining: Are study procedures likely to cause psychological distress? Are data adequately protected? Are participants informed of risks and benefits? Are confidentiality safeguards adequate? Only after IRB approval can research begin.

### Informed Consent: The Foundation of Ethical Participation

Informed consent means participants voluntarily agree to participate based on honest, complete information about what participation entails. It's not a document alone but a process: participants must understand risks, benefits, procedures, alternatives, and their rights before agreeing.

**The Informed Consent Form (ICF)**: Typically contains 10 key sections:

1. **Study Title and Purpose**: What is this research about? The ICF would state the study's aim clearly, e.g., "This study examines how organizational culture affects team outcomes."

2. **Researcher Identification**: Who is conducting the research? What institution? Contact information for questions.

3. **Procedures and Duration**: What will participants do? "You will participate in two interviews, each lasting 60-90 minutes, at a location of your choice. Interviews will be audio recorded with your consent."

4. **Risk Assessment**: What are potential harms? "Interviews discuss your workplace experiences, which might trigger anxiety or discomfort. You may experience emotional reactions recalling difficult experiences. Discussing concerns about leadership might create social risk if your identity were revealed (though we maintain confidentiality)."

5. **Benefits**: What might participants gain? "Findings may help organizations develop programs supporting team safety and engagement. You'll contribute to understanding how to create better workplaces." (Be honest: don't promise direct benefits if unlikely.)

6. **Confidentiality and Data Protection**: How will data be protected? "Interviews are audio-recorded, transcribed, and stored on encrypted servers. All identifying information is removed; transcripts use participant ID numbers, not names. Only the research team accesses recordings; they'll be deleted after study completion. Publications will never identify individuals."

7. **Voluntary Participation and Right to Withdraw**: Participation is optional and can be ended anytime. "Your participation is completely voluntary. You may refuse to answer any question or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Withdrawal won't affect your employment or relationship with leadership."

8. **Compensation** (if any): Are participants paid? "You will receive [compensation] for your time." (Compensation should be modest, not coercive.)

9. **Alternative Procedures**: Are there other participation options? "You may choose to participate in interviews only (without surveys), or provide written responses instead of audio interviews if you prefer."

10. **IRB Contact and Grievance**: How can participants raise concerns? "If you have questions about your rights as a research participant, contact the IRB at [contact]."

### CITI Training Requirement

Most institutions require researchers conducting human-subjects research to complete CITI (Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative) training—online modules on research ethics, IRB regulations, and participant protection. Training covers: (1) ethical conduct in research, (2) informed consent, (3) regulations governing human-subjects research (the Common Rule, the Belmont Report), and (4) special populations requiring extra protections. CITI certification documents that researchers understand ethical obligations before conducting research.

### Anonymity vs. Confidentiality: Critical Distinctions

**Anonymity**: Researcher never knows participants' identities. Truly anonymous data have no link between responses and individuals. An online survey where no login identifies respondents, responses collected without email or ID, and researchers receive only aggregate data benefits from true anonymity. Benefit: participants can't be identified even in principle. Drawback: can't contact participants for follow-up or member checking.

**Confidentiality**: Researcher knows identities but keeps them secret and protects linked data from disclosure. A researcher conducts interviews, meets participants (knows their identity), audio-records interaction, transcribes it, and removes names from transcripts. The transcript is confidential (only research team accesses it) but not anonymous (researcher knows which ID corresponds to whom).

**In Longitudinal Studies**: Anonymity is often impossible because you need to link pre-test to post-test data for the same person. Measuring each person before and after an intervention requires identifiable data (or at minimum, unique identifiers). Confidentiality—protecting identities while maintaining necessary data linkages—is the appropriate protection.

**In Cross-Sectional Studies**: If a study doesn't require follow-up, true anonymity is possible: respondents provide responses on anonymous surveys with no identifying information.

### Data Dissemination: Who Receives Data and How?

Ethical research clearly specifies how data will be shared:

**Internal Sharing**: Who within the research team accesses raw data? The ICF would specify that only core researchers access identifiable recordings and transcripts; research assistants work with de-identified coded data only.

**External Sharing**: Will data be shared with organizations or funders? In what form? The ICF should disclose: "Organization leadership will receive aggregate findings about team dynamics, not individual responses. Your individual data will never be shared; they will see only group-level summaries and themes." This prevents organizations from identifying individual participants and potential retaliation.

**Publication and Dissemination Steps**:
- Raw data (recordings, transcripts with names) are stored confidentially, accessed only by analysis team
- De-identified coded data (segments labeled with themes, not names) are used for analysis
- Aggregate findings (percentage of codes by theme, summary of themes, no individual identifiers) are reported in publications
- Direct quotes might be used only if sufficiently de-identified and with explicit consent

**Data Retention and Deletion**: How long are data kept? When destroyed? The ICF might state: "Audio recordings will be deleted after transcription and analysis is complete, within 2 years of study completion. De-identified transcripts may be retained longer for archival purposes. You may request your data be deleted at any time."

### Special Protections for Vulnerable Populations

If research involves vulnerable populations (minors, prisoners, cognitively impaired individuals, economically disadvantaged groups), additional protections apply. While organizational professionals aren't typically considered vulnerable, power dynamics (hierarchical organizations, management presence) require vigilance: participants might fear retaliation for expressing concerns. IRB protocols address this through independent recruitment (not by supervisors), guaranteed confidentiality, and assurances that refusal won't affect employment.
