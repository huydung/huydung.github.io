# Research Design Fundamentals

Research design is the overall strategy and structured plan that guides how you answer your research question. It encompasses the logical sequence from defining your question, positing hypotheses, reviewing literature, and then selecting the most appropriate methods. A sound design answers four critical questions: Is this the right study to answer my RQ? Am I consulting the right sources and populations? Will my approach produce credible findings? Are my methods relevant and timely to the problem?

Every research design comprises essential components: (1) a clear Research Question that defines what you seek to understand; (2) hypothesis or propositions spelling out expected relationships; (3) identification of Independent Variables (what you manipulate or predict from) and Dependent Variables (what you measure as outcomes); (4) specification of population, setting, and participants; (5) instrumentation (surveys, interviews, observation protocols); and (6) analysis plan (statistics, thematic coding, etc.). Understanding these components and how they interrelate enables researchers to design studies that produce credible, relevant evidence.

## Research Question (RQ)

A research question articulates what you want to understand—specific, answerable, and grounded in a gap in knowledge. It guides every methodological choice afterward. For example, in organizational psychology, a strong RQ might be: "What leader behaviors increase perceived psychological safety among team members in hierarchical organizational cultures?" This directly addresses a theoretical puzzle: Western research shows that hierarchy can undermine safety, yet some hierarchical organizations foster both safety and high performance. A weak RQ is vague ("How do leaders matter?"); a strong RQ is targeted and testable.

## Hypothesis & Propositions

Hypotheses predict expected relationships before data collection, grounded in theory and prior research. They provide falsifiable statements that guide analysis. An example hypothesis: "Leaders who explicitly acknowledge interdependence will increase team members' psychological safety" (testable via intervention). By contrast, exploratory-qualitative research uses propositions rather than strict hypotheses—research questions like "What mechanisms explain how leaders build trust in high-power-distance contexts?" seek to understand the "how" and "why" before measuring relationships numerically.

## Variables: IV, DV, Mediators, Moderators

Independent Variables (IVs) are what you manipulate (experimental) or predict from (correlational). Dependent Variables (DVs) are outcomes you measure. For example, in a game studio study, leader training or cultural practices might be IVs; team safety and performance might be DVs. Mediators explain the mechanism (why does IV affect DV?), while moderators affect the strength or direction of that relationship (does the IV→DV link differ by context or group?). Confounding or extraneous variables must be controlled to avoid spurious results.

## Population, Setting & Participants

Who are you studying, where, and under what conditions? A study of psychological safety in video game studios examines people (developers, designers, leaders), a setting (game development environments), and conditions (crunch periods, collaborative phases, etc.). The setting shapes findings—results from established studios may not generalize to indie startups, and findings from tech hubs like Ho Chi Minh City may differ from remote or rural contexts. Clearly specifying these parameters ensures findings are trustworthy and applicably defined.

## Instrumentation & Measurement

How will you collect data? Quantitative studies use validated scales (surveys, questionnaires); qualitative studies use interviews, observation protocols, or document analysis. Instruments should be valid (measuring what they claim) and reliable (doing so consistently). For example, a psychological safety survey should ask about felt freedom to speak up, comfort with dissent, and trust—multiple items that converge on the construct. An interview guide for understanding safety in game development should include open-ended questions allowing rich narrative.

## Analysis Plan

How will you make sense of data once collected? Quantitative designs specify statistical tests (e.g., multiple regression, structural equation modeling to test relationships); qualitative designs specify coding methods (e.g., thematic analysis to identify patterns). A strong analysis plan is determined before data collection to avoid cherry-picking findings. For example, a quantitative study should pre-specify which variables are primary hypotheses (tested with stricter significance levels) versus exploratory. Qualitative studies should pre-specify coding approaches (inductive vs. deductive codes) and decision rules for theme saturation.
