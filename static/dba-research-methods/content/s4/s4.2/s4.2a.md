# Quantitative Designs

Quantitative research reduces phenomena to numbers, enabling statistical testing and generalization. Designs range along a spectrum from descriptive (what is the current state?) to predictive (what variables relate to outcomes?) to prescriptive (what actions produce desired results?) to causal (does X cause Y?). Each level requires progressively stronger control and design rigor. The choice reflects your research question and practical constraints: experimental designs offer the strongest evidence of causation but are difficult in organizations; correlational designs are feasible and still highly useful for prediction and understanding relationships.

Quantitative designs rest on measurement, randomization (where possible), and statistical inference. All quantitative work shares common elements: operationalized variables (defined precisely and measured numerically), sample sizes calculated for statistical power, and pre-specified analysis plans to minimize bias. The trade-off is always between internal validity (confidence in causal claims within your sample) and external validity (ability to generalize to broader populations).

## Experimental Designs

Experimental designs feature random assignment of participants to control and treatment groups, manipulation of the independent variable, and pre/post measurement. They provide the strongest internal validity and allow you to claim causation: "X causes Y." Example: A software company tests a new leadership development program by randomly assigning teams to receive training (treatment) or not (control), measuring team performance before and after. If treatment teams improved significantly more than control teams, you can credibly claim the intervention caused the change. The cost is feasibility: randomization is often impossible in organizations, and manipulation may be unethical or impractical.

## Quasi-Experimental Designs

Quasi-experimental designs lack random assignment but retain pre/post measurement and comparison groups, often using pre-existing groups (e.g., two company offices, one receiving training, one not). Internal validity is lower than true experiments because you cannot rule out that pre-existing differences between groups explain outcomes. Causation cannot be definitively claimed—selection bias is a threat. However, quasi-experiments are more feasible in organizational contexts and can still provide useful evidence, especially when combined with statistical controls (e.g., ANCOVA, matching). Example: A game studio measures team safety and performance before and after implementing psychological safety training across different regional teams, acknowledging that pre-existing cultural differences between regions may affect outcomes.

## Non-Experimental (Correlational) Designs

Non-experimental designs, comprising over 90% of organizational and social science research, measure variables without manipulation and test relationships statistically (correlation, regression, structural equation modeling). You cannot claim causation, but you can describe relationships, predict outcomes, and understand associations in real-world settings. Example: Measuring leadership style and team performance across multiple software companies and finding they correlate positively—the opposite of what some Western theory predicts—enables discovering that context matters. This design is practical (no intervention required), ethical, and yields actionable insights, though it cannot prove that leadership style *causes* performance (reverse causality or unmeasured confounds are possible).

## Spectrum: Descriptive → Predictive → Prescriptive → Causal

Quantitative research progresses along a maturity spectrum. **Descriptive studies** characterize a population (e.g., "What is average psychological safety in Vietnamese tech companies?"). **Predictive studies** identify variables that forecast outcomes (e.g., "Does leader credibility predict team performance?"). **Prescriptive studies** recommend actions (e.g., "Implement mentoring programs to boost psychological safety"). **Causal studies** prove X causes Y (e.g., experimental leadership training increases performance), the gold standard but hardest to achieve. Effective research often moves along this spectrum: descriptive findings identify phenomena, predictive work reveals relationships, qualitative investigation explains mechanisms, and experiments test interventions—together building strong evidence for change.
