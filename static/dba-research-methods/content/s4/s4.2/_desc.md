# Design Types

Research designs fall into broad families—quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods—each with distinct strengths and trade-offs. Quantitative designs (experimental, quasi-experimental, non-experimental correlational) excel at testing hypotheses, measuring relationships numerically, and generalizing findings to larger populations. Qualitative designs (case study, phenomenological, grounded theory, ethnography) emphasize depth, context, and understanding meaning through rich narrative and emergent themes. The choice between them depends on your research question: "Does X predict Y?" calls for quantitative methods; "What does it mean to experience X?" calls for qualitative exploration.

Selecting a design requires clarity on your aims. If you need to demonstrate causation with high internal validity, an experimental design with random assignment and control groups is gold standard—but often impractical or unethical in organizational contexts. If you need to understand how and why phenomena occur in real-world settings, qualitative designs are more appropriate. Strong research integrates multiple design types: quantitative methods establish relationships across many people, while qualitative work reveals the mechanisms and context explaining those relationships, together providing both breadth and depth.
