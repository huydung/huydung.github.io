# Variables & Models

A variable is anything that varies or differs across people, groups, or time—age, leadership style, psychological safety, job performance. Independent Variables (IVs) are what you manipulate (in experiments) or predict from (in correlational studies); Dependent Variables (DVs) are the outcomes you measure. Mediator and moderator variables add sophistication to models, answering deeper questions: "Why does X affect Y?" (mediation) and "Under what conditions does X affect Y?" (moderation). Understanding these relationships and their mechanisms is central to research impact—it moves from simple prediction to actionable insight.

For example, in research on team dynamics, leadership style might be an IV (predictor); team performance might be a DV (outcome). Understanding whether trust mediates that relationship (Why does leadership style matter? Because it builds trust) informs precise recommendations: focus leadership development on trust-building behaviors rather than generic "be a better leader" advice.

## Independent Variable (IV)

An Independent Variable is what you hypothesize influences an outcome. In experiments, you manipulate it; in correlational studies, you measure it and predict from it. IVs are "causes" in your theoretical model, though correlation cannot prove causation. Example: In studying organizational culture, cultural practices (transparent communication, inclusive decision-making) would be an IV. In training effectiveness research, the training itself (exposure vs. control) would be the manipulated IV. Multiple IVs can exist in one study: cultural practices, leader credibility, and organizational size might all be IVs predicting safety and performance outcomes.

## Dependent Variable (DV)

A Dependent Variable is the outcome you measure, hypothesized to depend on the IV. DVs are "effects" in your theoretical model. Example: In the organizational culture study, psychological safety and team performance would be DVs—the outcomes predicted by cultural practices and leadership. In training research, post-training measures of safety or engagement would be the DV. A study can have multiple DVs; the key is that they are measured after (or simultaneously with) the IV and are theoretically downstream of it.

## Mediator Variable

A mediator explains *how* or *why* an IV affects a DV—the mechanism. It is influenced by the IV and in turn influences the DV. Example: Suppose the mechanism is: Leadership credibility → Trust → Psychological Safety. The leadership credibility cultural context may lead employees to respect and trust leaders who clearly communicate and follow through, and that credibility-based trust then enables psychological safety—safety emerges through trusted leadership. Identifying mediators is powerful: it tells organizations exactly what to build (leader credibility, trustworthy relationships) rather than just that cultural factors matter. This insight transforms generic "improve your culture" advice into actionable specifics: develop practices that increase leader transparency and consistency.

## Moderator Variable

A moderator affects the *strength* or *direction* of the IV→DV relationship—it changes the slope. "Does the IV→DV link differ depending on the moderator?" Example: Suppose organizational size moderates the leadership credibility → psychological safety relationship: in small teams (15 people), credibility might not strongly predict safety (teams already know leaders personally), but in large organizations (100+ people), credibility might strongly predict safety (leader authority structures communication). Another example: Industry sector might moderate—game studios with high crunch culture might show different relationships between leadership practices and safety than more stable tech companies. Moderators don't explain the mechanism (that's mediation); they specify boundary conditions.

## Extraneous & Intervening Variables

Extraneous variables are confounds—factors unrelated to your theory but that might influence the DV and muddy conclusions. Example: In studying leadership and safety, employee tenure or job stress might correlate with both leadership perceptions and safety, creating spurious associations. You control extraneous variables through study design (matching groups, random assignment) or statistical methods (ANCOVA, regression with controls). Intervening variables are theoretically important but unmeasured variables that might lie between IV and DV. Example: Suppose leadership credibility → organizational trust → psychological safety is the true pathway, but you only measure leadership and safety. Trust is an unmeasured intervening variable, and ignoring it misspecifies your model.

## Pitfalls: Don't Start with Mediator, Avoid Complexity

A common error is "mediation first"—hypothesizing a mediator before establishing that the IV predicts the DV. Example: Don't start with "Trust mediates leadership→safety" if you haven't first shown that leadership predicts safety. Build your model step-by-step: (1) does IV predict DV? (2) if yes, what explains it (mediator)? Another pitfall is over-complexity: including so many mediators, moderators, and controls that the model becomes unwieldy and uninterpretable. Occam's Razor applies: the simplest model that fits the data and theory is preferable. Effective research typically tests a core model (leadership→safety, safety→performance) with meaningful control variables, avoiding a "everything predicts everything" hairball.
