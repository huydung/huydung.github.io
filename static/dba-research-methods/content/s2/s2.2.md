## Problem Statement

A research problem is a gap, tension, or contradiction in knowledge or practice that warrants investigation. It answers: Why does this question matter? Why should anyone care? A strong problem statement grounds your research in relevance and urgency, distinguishing genuine investigation from idle curiosity. For example, the problem might not be merely "How do teams communicate?" but rather "Western organizational theory predicts that flat hierarchies enable communication, yet emerging research in some cultural contexts suggests otherwise—creating a theoretical contradiction that demands resolution." This specificity transforms a general interest into a researchable problem.

## Relevance to the Field
Your problem must address a gap that matters to scholars, practitioners, or both. Is there a contradiction between existing theories? An under-studied population or context? A practical challenge that research could illuminate? A problem is highly relevant if solving it would change how organizations operate or how scholars understand phenomena.

## Uniqueness & Originality
The problem should not simply repeat existing studies but should extend, challenge, or apply knowledge in a novel way. Your work is original if no prior quantitative study has tested a particular relationship in a specific population, or if your qualitative exploration addresses a previously unstudied context.

## Feasibility & Manageability
Can you actually investigate this problem? Do you have access to relevant participants, data, or settings? Does the scope fit your resources and timeline? A feasible problem is one you can reasonably investigate within your constraints.

## Clear & Specific Definition
Define the core constructs and context precisely. Vague problems ("How do teams work better?") are not researchable. Specific problems ("How do flat versus hierarchical organizational structures interact with leadership transparency to predict team innovation in software development teams?") can be investigated empirically. Clearly identify the variables or phenomena, the population, and the context.

## Alignment with Field Advancement
Evaluate whether your problem advances the field meaningfully. Does it contribute new knowledge? Does it improve understanding or practice? A strong problem advances knowledge by testing whether assumptions hold universally or require cultural modification, or by improving organizational practices.

## Practical Significance & Impact
Consider whether solving this problem will have practical value. Who will benefit? How will findings change practice or understanding? If your hypothesis is supported, how might organizations change their approaches? This practical impact strengthens the problem's significance.

## Background Study Functions: Establishing Relevance
The background study—conducted through literature review and contextual analysis—demonstrates that this problem matters. Show: (1) the phenomenon you're studying is important to outcomes people care about; (2) the population or context you're investigating is significant; (3) existing theory and practice are insufficient to address this problem.

## Background Study Functions: Discussing Evolution
Trace how understanding of the problem has evolved. Show: How has thinking about organizational structure progressed? How has understanding of leadership changed? Have researchers begun questioning universal assumptions? Showing evolution positions the problem as timely.

## Background Study Functions: Explaining Gaps
Explicitly name the gaps your problem addresses. Examples: (1) lack of quantitative data on X in population Y; (2) limited research on intervention Z in context W; (3) underrepresentation of industry or organizational type in scholarly research. Naming gaps makes the problem's necessity clear.

## Background Study Functions: Defending Methodology
Your problem statement should foreshadow why your chosen methods are appropriate. If your problem requires understanding *how* leaders build team cohesion, qualitative methods are justified. If it requires testing a specific relationship across a population, quantitative methods are justified. If it requires both breadth and depth, mixed methods are justified.
