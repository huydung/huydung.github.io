## Quantitative Research Questions

Quantitative research questions ask "what" and "how much," seeking to identify, measure, or predict relationships between variables. They are framed to be testable through statistical analysis, with clear independent and dependent variables. The structure is typically predictive or comparative: "Does X predict Y?" "Is there a relationship between X and Y?" "Does X differ significantly from Y?" These questions assume that reality can be measured numerically and that statistical inference reveals patterns or relationships.

## Asks "What" and "How Much"
Quantitative RQs focus on measurable phenomena and numerical relationships. They ask: What is the prevalence? How strong is the correlation? What is the difference in means? For example: "Does organizational transparency positively predict team innovation among game developers?" This is clearly a "what" question (Does it?) combined with "how much" (What is the strength of the relationship?).

## Identifies Measurable Independent & Dependent Variables
Independent variables (IVs) are the predictors or causes you are testing. Dependent variables (DVs) are the outcomes you are measuring. They must be operationalized—converted into measurable indicators or scales. For example, organizational transparency (IV) might be measured using a validated organizational assessment scale. Innovation (DV) might be measured using team performance metrics or innovation metrics. The question explicitly states which variable predicts which.

## Testable Through Statistical Analysis
Can you analyze this question using statistics? Can you collect numerical data, compute descriptive statistics, test significance, estimate effect sizes? A researchable RQ means you can clearly articulate the data you'd collect and the statistical method you'd use. If you can't imagine the data and analysis, the question isn't testable.

## Clean Single-Relationship Focus
Quantitative RQs are strongest when they target one primary relationship or comparison, avoiding unnecessary complexity. Weak: "How do organizational structure, leadership style, organizational culture, and individual personality all interact to affect team innovation?" This sprawls across multiple variables. Strong: "Does transparent organizational structure predict team innovation?" This is clean, focused, and answerable.

## Directionality & Prediction
Quantitative RQs can be non-directional ("Is there a relationship?") or directional ("Does X positively predict Y?"). Directional questions are stronger because they reflect theoretical expectation or informed prediction. This directional framing makes hypothesis testing sharper and effect interpretation more meaningful.

## Population Specification
Always specify the population in your quantitative RQ. Comparing results across populations (e.g., software developers vs. game developers) requires separate RQs or conditional framing. Specifying population allows you to claim findings are specific to that group and not overgeneralized.
