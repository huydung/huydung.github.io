## Hypothesis (Quantitative)

A hypothesis is a specific, testable prediction about the relationship between variables, formulated before data collection on the basis of theory, prior research, or logical reasoning. Hypotheses are central to quantitative research, guiding statistical testing and enabling researchers to make claims about causation, correlation, or difference. They reflect the researcher's informed expectations and allow the data to confirm, refute, or complicate those expectations. A strong hypothesis is clear about constructs, specifies directionality, identifies the population, and articulates the statistical logic by which it will be tested.

## Independent Variable (IV) Manipulated or Measured
The independent variable is the predictor or cause you are testing. In experimental designs, the IV is actively manipulated (e.g., assigning participants to a leadership training vs. control condition). In correlational designs, the IV is measured through survey or assessment (e.g., measuring existing organizational transparency without manipulation). Hypotheses must clearly state what IV you are predicting from.

## Dependent Variable (DV) Measured as Outcome
The dependent variable is the outcome or criterion you are predicting. It is always measured, not manipulated. In an example about organizational structure and team innovation, innovation is the DV—measured using performance metrics or team assessments. The hypothesis specifies the relationship: Does the IV predict the DV? By how much? In what direction? The DV must be clearly measurable.

## Null Hypothesis (H₀) vs. Alternative Hypothesis (Hₐ)
Statistical testing relies on two hypotheses. The null hypothesis (H₀) assumes no relationship or no significant difference: "Organizational transparency does not predict team innovation" or "β = 0." The alternative hypothesis (Hₐ) predicts a relationship or difference: "Organizational transparency predicts team innovation" or "β ≠ 0." Statistical tests evaluate whether data provide sufficient evidence to reject the null in favor of the alternative.

## Directional vs. Non-Directional Hypotheses
Directional hypotheses predict the *direction* of a relationship: "Organizational transparency *positively* predicts team innovation (β > 0)." Non-directional hypotheses predict only that a relationship exists, without specifying direction: "Organizational transparency predicts team innovation (β ≠ 0)." Directional hypotheses are stronger because they reflect theoretical expectation and enable one-tailed statistical tests.

## Moderation & Conditional Hypotheses
Some hypotheses propose that a third variable moderates the relationship between IV and DV. Example: "Organizational transparency predicts team innovation, but this relationship is stronger for teams with experienced leaders than inexperienced leaders." This conditional hypothesis reflects interaction effects. Qualitative work can explore such moderation by examining conditions under which factors affect outcomes differently.

## Must Be Testable, Specific, & Falsifiable
A hypothesis is scientific only if it can be tested empirically and potentially proven wrong. Untestable: "Good leadership is essential" (subjective, no empirical test). Testable: "Leadership transparency is associated with higher team innovation scores than leadership that lacks transparency" (can be measured and compared). A hypothesis must be falsifiable—it must be possible for data to contradict it.

## Evaluation: Clear Constructs
Before formulating a hypothesis, operationalize your constructs—define them in measurable terms. What exactly do you mean by each variable? Clear operationalization ensures the hypothesis targets the right phenomena. For example, is organizational transparency measured by employee surveys, leader surveys, or documented communication patterns? Define it clearly.

## Evaluation: Specified Population
The hypothesis should identify the population to which it applies. "Transparency predicts innovation" (too general, applies nowhere specifically). "Organizational transparency positively predicts team innovation among software developers at mid-sized studios" (specific, allowing assessment of generalizability). This specificity is important.

## Evaluation: Statistical Methods Articulated
Your hypothesis should align with the statistical method you will use to test it. If you hypothesize a relationship between two continuous variables, correlation or regression is appropriate. If you hypothesize differences between groups, ANOVA or t-tests are appropriate. The hypothesis structure and analysis method should align clearly.
