## Quantitative Data: Discrete & Continuous

Quantitative data are numerical values expressing measurements, counts, or scaled responses that can be analyzed using statistical methods. Quantitative data enable researchers to identify patterns, test hypotheses, and generalize findings to larger populations. Example: A survey administered to 150 software developers collecting Likert-scale responses (1-5 agreement scales) on psychological safety, team cohesion, and job satisfaction. These numerical responses are aggregated, analyzed using correlation or regression, and expressed as means, correlations, and effect sizes to test theoretical relationships.

## Discrete Data

Discrete data are countable values that exist only as whole numbers—you cannot have 2.5 employees or 3.7 projects completed. Common discrete measures include: number of team members, number of projects completed, count of safety incidents, or number of training sessions attended. In organizational contexts, researchers might count the number of teams receiving training or the number of safety-related incidents reported. Discrete data are analyzed using frequency counts, proportions, and non-parametric statistical tests.

## Continuous Data

Continuous data are measurable on a scale with infinite possible values between any two points—salary, project duration, engagement scores, or response latency. Likert-scale responses (e.g., 1–5 rating of safety agreement) are technically ordinal but often treated as continuous in statistical analysis when sufficient scale points exist. Survey data—respondents rating statements like "I feel safe speaking up in meetings" on a 5-point scale—are treated as continuous to enable parametric analyses and advanced statistics. Continuous data support correlation analysis, regression, and ANOVA.

## Statistical Expression and Analysis

All quantitative data are ultimately expressed as numbers and analyzed statistically to test relationships, compare groups, or identify trends. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations) summarize the data; inferential statistics test hypotheses about populations. In a structural equation modeling analysis, continuous survey responses are aggregated into latent constructs and analyzed to determine whether predicted relationships hold true in a particular organizational context.
