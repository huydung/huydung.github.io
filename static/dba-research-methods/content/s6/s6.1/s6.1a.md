## Core: Data vs. Information, Types by Collection Source

Data are raw, unprocessed facts—observations, measurements, or recorded events that exist independent of interpretation. Information is data that has been processed, organized, and contextualized to answer questions or support insights. Understanding this distinction is critical for research design: raw survey responses are data, but analyzed themes are information. Researchers must recognize that data collection is not merely gathering facts, but laying groundwork for the interpretive work that transforms facts into meaningful evidence.

## Data Collection Sources

Data collection sources fall into four primary categories, each with distinct advantages:

### Observational
Observational data arise from directly watching or recording behavior without intervention. In studying team dynamics, researchers observe team interactions, meeting communication patterns, decision-making processes, and informal interactions to capture how teams actually function. Observational data preserve ecological validity but require significant time investment and trained observers to ensure reliability.

### Experimental
Experimental data emerge from controlled interventions where variables are manipulated and outcomes measured. A company might test a leadership development program by assigning teams randomly to receive training (treatment) or not (control), then measure team performance before and after. Experimental designs yield causal inferences but may sacrifice realism due to laboratory or structured conditions.

### Simulation
Simulation data come from modeling systems or scenarios computationally to explore "what-if" questions. While less common in organizational research, simulations could model team dynamics or predict outcomes under different conditions. Simulations offer control and efficiency but depend on model validity.

### Derived
Derived data result from synthesizing, transforming, or aggregating existing data from multiple sources. Combining quantitative survey results with qualitative interview themes creates derived insights that neither data source alone provides. Derived data can be powerful but require transparent documentation of transformation logic.

## Primary vs. Secondary Data

### Primary Data
Primary data are collected directly by the researcher for the specific study. Original surveys administered to employees and semi-structured interviews with team members are primary data—original information gathered for your specific research question. Primary data are costly to collect but offer high control over measurement and context.

### Secondary Data
Secondary data are pre-existing information gathered for other purposes but re-analyzed for the current research question. Organizational records (HR data, team performance metrics, project timelines), published literature, or industry reports on team dynamics represent secondary data sources. Secondary data save resources but may not perfectly align with current research needs.
