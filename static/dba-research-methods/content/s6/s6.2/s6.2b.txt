## Qualitative: Six Sources of Evidence

Qualitative research draws evidence from multiple sources—not just interviews, but documentation, observations, participant immersion, and physical artifacts—each offering distinct insights into organizational phenomena. These six sources can be used independently or combined through triangulation to strengthen credibility. Case study research, in particular, leverages multiple sources to develop holistic understanding of complex organizational systems.

## 1. Documentation

Documentation includes written records, reports, memos, emails, policies, and published materials created by the organization or community being studied. Documents provide historical perspective and capture organizational intent—for example, an organization's psychological safety policy or team meeting minutes revealing how safety is (or isn't) prioritized. Documentary evidence is non-reactive—researchers review what was already created for other purposes—and provides traceable records. Research could draw on organizational training materials, post-mortems from completed projects (which often identify dynamics and challenges), or HR policies on speaking up and conflict resolution.

## 2. Archival Records

Archival records are systematically organized historical data held in organizational repositories: personnel records, financial records, project timelines, incident reports, and performance evaluations. These records document organizational patterns over time and provide objective evidence against which participant claims can be checked. Organizational archival records might include project delivery dates (indicating whether development practices affected timelines), employee turnover by team (suggesting psychological safety impact), or disciplinary records (showing whether interventions reduced unnecessary escalations). Archival data are reliable but may be incomplete or only available at aggregate levels due to privacy constraints.

## 3. Interviews

Interviews—particularly semi-structured and unstructured formats—are direct conversations with participants designed to capture their interpretations, experiences, and perspectives on phenomena. Unlike surveys, interviews allow researchers to adapt questions, probe responses, and explore unexpected tangents that reveal deeper insight. Example: Semi-structured interviews with software developers, using a guide of core questions about psychological safety, leadership, and organizational culture, but allowing conversation to flow naturally. Interviews are time-intensive but yield rich, contextual data unavailable through surveys.

## 4. Direct Observation

Direct observation involves a researcher systematically watching events, interactions, or environments without participating, recording field notes on what unfolds. Example: An observer watching a team meeting might document who speaks, how ideas are received, whether silence follows critical comments, and how leaders respond to dissent—all revealing psychological safety in action. Direct observation preserves ecological validity (people behave naturally, not in artificial survey contexts) but requires trained observers to ensure reliability and may alter behavior if participants know they're being watched.

## 5. Participant Observation

Participant observation involves a researcher embedded in the community or organization, simultaneously participating in activities and observing dynamics from within. The researcher attends meetings, works alongside team members, and reflects on their own experiences alongside external observations. This intensive immersion provides deep cultural insight—understanding unspoken norms, informal power structures, and the lived reality of team dynamics. Participant observation yields holistic understanding but requires extended time commitment and reflexivity about the researcher's influence on the group.

## 6. Physical Artifacts

Physical artifacts are tangible objects, environments, or systems that reflect organizational values and practices: office layout, meeting room equipment, communication platforms, training videos, or team rituals. An organization's physical setup—open plan vs. private spaces, locations of informal gathering areas—reflects assumptions about collaboration and safety. Digital artifacts matter equally: communication channels for ideas, suggestion systems (or their absence), comment threads showing how team members interact. Even product design decisions can reveal organizational values—design emphasizing innovation signals safety for experimentation, while risk-averse design signals fear of failure. These physical traces provide unobtrusive evidence of organizational culture.

## Integration Through Triangulation

Qualitative researchers often triangulate—combining data from multiple sources to strengthen credibility and develop richer interpretation. A study combining interview data (what people say they value) with observation (how they actually behave) and documentary evidence (what the organization claims to value) yields more robust understanding than any single source. Integration of quantitative findings (which variables predict outcomes) with qualitative themes (explaining why those relationships exist) and observational data (documenting actual organizational behavior) exemplifies triangulation across methods, providing comprehensive evidence.
