# Sampling Core Concepts

Sampling begins with a clear understanding of three linked concepts: the target population (the broader group you want to understand), the sample (the subset you actually study), and generalization (the inference from sample findings back to the population). The target population is defined by inclusion criteria ("software developers in Southeast Asia with 1+ year in game development") and sets the boundary for what you claim your findings mean. The sample is who you recruit and study; ideally, the sample resembles the population so findings generalize. Sampling ratio (sample size divided by population size) and representativeness are key metrics: larger samples and closer resemblance to the population yield more confident generalization.

Understanding the population → sample → generalization pipeline clarifies why sampling matters. In studying leadership and safety across multiple tech companies, the target population might be "software development professionals in Southeast Asia." The sample might be 150 developers at several established companies plus smaller studios. Because the sample includes diverse company sizes and roles (to ensure reasonable diversity), findings likely generalize to similar Southeast Asian tech contexts. But if the sample included only large established companies, findings might not represent indie developers. Similarly, a qualitative study interviewing 6 developers aims not for statistical generalization but for rich thematic insight relevant to the experience-based research question.

## Target Population vs. Accessible Population vs. Sample

The target population is the ideal group you want to understand (e.g., "all software developers in game studios across Southeast Asia"). The accessible population is who you can realistically reach (e.g., "developers at known game studios in Ho Chi Minh City, Hanoi, and Bangkok," a subset of the target). Your sample is who you actually recruit and study (e.g., "150 developers who completed surveys at these studios"). Generalization is strongest when the accessible population resembles the target population—if you sample from major studios but aim to claim findings about all game developers, you must acknowledge that your sampled studios are likely larger, more established, and possibly higher-paid than indie developers, potentially limiting generalizability.

## Participants vs. Subjects vs. Respondents

Terminology varies by discipline and method. "Participants" is the modern, respectful term for people in research, emphasizing agency and consent. "Subjects" is older terminology, sometimes used in experimental contexts. "Respondents" often refers to survey participants specifically. The shift in language reflects ethical evolution: researchers now emphasize that participants are not passive objects of study but active contributors to knowledge creation.

## Sampling Ratio

Sampling ratio is the proportion of the population included in the sample: sample size ÷ population size. Example: If the target population of game developers in the region is estimated at 5,000 and you sampled 150, the sampling ratio is 150/5000 = 3%. A higher sampling ratio generally yields more confident generalization, though the relationship is not linear—a sample of 300 from 5,000 (6% ratio) is more than twice as powerful as 150. In practice, researchers aim for sampling ratios of 10-20% for population surveys, but organizational and practical constraints often limit this. A 3% ratio suggests findings apply to similar studios but should be cautiously generalized.

## Representativeness

Representativeness means the sample resembles the population on key demographic and substantive characteristics. A representative sample reduces bias and strengthens generalization. Example: If the software development workforce is 70% male and 30% female, a representative sample would approximate that split. If the target population spans diverse experience levels (junior to senior), the sample should too. Representativeness is checked through demographic comparisons (sample vs. known population statistics) and statistical tests. Non-probability sampling methods (convenience, quota) compromise representativeness but are practical in organizational contexts; probability methods (random, stratified) enhance it but require a complete sampling frame.

## Sample Size Considerations

Sample size reflects a trade-off between statistical power (ability to detect true effects), practical feasibility, and cost. Quantitative research uses power analysis to determine adequate sample size (usually 80% power to detect meaningful effects). A sample of 150 developers likely yields 80%+ power for regression analysis with multiple predictors. Qualitative research uses saturation rather than power: the sample size is adequate when new interviews stop revealing new themes. Six to twelve participants are typical for saturation, though complexity affects this. The art is balancing rigor (larger samples) with feasibility (time, cost, participant availability).
