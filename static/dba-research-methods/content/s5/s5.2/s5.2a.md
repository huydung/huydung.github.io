# Quantitative Sampling Techniques

Quantitative sampling prioritizes representativeness and generalizability, using probability methods that assign each population member a known chance of selection. These methods—simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster—support statistical inference: if your sample is unbiased and large enough, findings apply to the broader population with quantifiable confidence (e.g., "95% confident that the true population effect is between ±5% of our sample estimate"). The trade-off is feasibility: probability methods require a complete sampling frame (a list of all population members) and careful implementation to avoid bias. Non-probability methods (convenience, purposive, snowball, quota) sacrifice representativeness for practicality, used when a sampling frame is unavailable or when you need specific types of participants.

For quantitative research in organizations, mixing probability and non-probability methods is common. Stratified probability sampling within a company (ensuring representation across levels, teams, tenures) enables statistical generalization, while non-probability refinement (HR recruiting participants meeting diversity criteria) boosts feasibility. This hybrid approach supports both statistical generalization and practical success.

## Probability Sampling Methods

**Simple random sampling**: Every population member has an equal, known chance (e.g., 1 in 50) of selection. Use a random number generator to select participants from a complete list. Example: A company maintains a roster of 500 developers; randomly select 150 by generating 150 unique random numbers between 1 and 500. Simple random sampling is unbiased but requires a sampling frame and may miss important subgroups (e.g., if women are 20% of the population, a small random sample might randomly include only 10% women, introducing sampling error).

**Systematic sampling**: Select every nth person from a population list (e.g., every 3rd person). Example: List all 500 developers, then recruit every 3rd person on the list (positions 3, 6, 9, 12, etc.). Systematic sampling is convenient and reduces bias compared to convenience sampling, though it can introduce periodicity bias if the list has hidden patterns (e.g., if every 3rd person has a particular characteristic).

**Stratified sampling**: Divide the population into strata (subgroups) and randomly sample within each stratum. Example: Stratify 500 developers by level (100 junior, 250 mid, 150 senior); then randomly sample 30% from each stratum (30 junior, 75 mid, 45 senior = 150 total). You can oversample underrepresented groups (e.g., sample 50% of the women to ensure their voice is heard, then weight results appropriately). Stratified sampling ensures key subgroups are represented and reduces sampling error, making it powerful for organizational contexts where meaningful subgroups exist.

**Cluster sampling**: Divide the population into clusters (e.g., five regional offices) and randomly select clusters to study, then sample within selected clusters. Example: Randomly select 3 of 5 regional offices, then recruit all developers in those 3 (or a sub-sample). Cluster sampling is efficient for geographically dispersed populations but less precise than stratified sampling (between-cluster differences may inflate error). It is often used in longitudinal research where studying entire clusters reduces logistics.

**Sampling with replacement vs. without**: With replacement, each sampled person is returned to the pool and could theoretically be selected again; without replacement (standard), once selected, a person cannot be selected again. Most organizational research uses without-replacement sampling to avoid duplicates.

## Non-Probability Sampling Methods

**Convenience sampling**: Recruit whoever is readily available—volunteers, people near the office, email respondents. Quick and cheap but highly biased: volunteers may be more engaged or have strong opinions, skewing results. Example: Emailing developers and asking whoever wants to complete a survey risks capturing only highly motivated employees. Convenience sampling is useful for exploratory or pilot work but inadequate for rigorous research aiming at generalization.

**Purposive sampling**: Deliberately select participants meeting predefined criteria relevant to your research question. Example: "Recruit 150 developers stratified by level (junior, mid, senior), tenure (0-2 years, 2-5 years, 5+ years), and role (programmer, designer, manager)" to ensure diversity and information-richness. Purposive sampling is practical in organizations, transparent (criteria are explicit), and often used in mixed-methods research. It does not guarantee representativeness but improves it and is more defensible than convenience sampling.

**Snowball sampling**: Ask initial participants to refer others, building a sample through chains of referral. Useful when recruiting hard-to-reach populations (e.g., people with specific experiences or perspectives). Example: Interview one developer who had experienced a safety-related incident, ask them to refer a colleague with similar experiences, continue until reaching desired sample size. Snowball sampling can create bias (referred participants are similar to referrers) and is best used as a supplement or in qualitative research where representativeness is less critical.

**Quota sampling**: Set quotas for subgroups and recruit until quotas are met. Example: "Recruit 50 junior, 50 mid, 50 senior developers" to match organizational proportions. Quotas ensure subgroup representation but introduce judgment bias in who is selected within each quota (a recruiter might unconsciously favor friendlier people). Quota sampling is practical but less rigorous than stratified random sampling.

## Mixed Sampling: Stratified + Simple Random

A hybrid approach combines stratification's subgroup assurance with simple random selection within strata, leveraging strengths of both. Example: Stratify 500 developers by level and tenure (creating, say, 12 strata), then randomly sample proportionally within each stratum. This ensures representation across key dimensions while maintaining randomness, reducing both bias and sampling error—often the practical gold standard in organizational research.

## Goal: Representativeness & Generalization

Quantitative sampling's ultimate aim is representativeness: the sample mirrors the population in key ways, so findings generalize confidently. Statistical power and effect size also matter: a large sample (e.g., 150) enables detecting subtle effects and reduces standard errors, allowing confident inference. Representative sampling + adequate sample size + careful analysis = trustworthy generalization from sample to population.
